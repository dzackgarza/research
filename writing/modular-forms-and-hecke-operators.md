# Modular forms, Hecke operators and L-functions {#sec-modular-forms-hecke}

Modular forms connect the geometry of modular curves with arithmetic data such
as point counts over finite fields. Hecke operators act on their finite-dimensional
spaces, and normalized eigenforms turn those operators into Fourier coefficients.
The same coefficients occur in Euler products. The boundary restrictions of
orthogonal modular forms provide a further connection with lattice classes;
see @sec-kneser-neighbors.

## Congruence subgroups and modular curves

For a positive integer $N$, the *principal congruence subgroup* is

$$
\Gamma(N)=\ker\bigl(\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)
\longrightarrow\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)\bigr).
$$

A *congruence subgroup* contains some $\Gamma(N)$. The same definition applies
to $\operatorname{SL}_n(\mathbb Z)$ using reduction modulo $N$. In rank two,
the principal examples between $\Gamma(N)$ and $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ are

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Gamma_0(N)&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}:
c\equiv0\pmod N\right\},\\
\Gamma_1(N)&=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}:
c\equiv0,\quad a\equiv d\equiv1\pmod N\right\}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Their indices in $\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ are, respectively,

$$
\begin{aligned}
[\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z):\Gamma(N)]
 &=N^3\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2}),\\
[\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z):\Gamma_1(N)]
 &=N^2\prod_{p\mid N}(1-p^{-2}),\\
[\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z):\Gamma_0(N)]
 &=N\prod_{p\mid N}(1+p^{-1}).
\end{aligned}
$$

These formulas follow from the reduction map and the successive quotients
$\Gamma_1(N)/\Gamma(N)\cong\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z$ and
$\Gamma_0(N)/\Gamma_1(N)\cong(\mathbb Z/N\mathbb Z)^\times$
[@DS05, §1.2]. Finite index also permits the Schreier construction of generators:
choose representatives $t$ for right cosets $\Gamma t$, including $1$.
For an ambient generator $s$, write $ts=ht'$ with $h\in\Gamma$ and $t'$
the representative of $\Gamma ts$. The elements $h=ts(t')^{-1}$ generate
$\Gamma$, since rewriting a word in the ambient generators gives a
telescoping product of these elements.

The analytic modular curve is $Y(\Gamma)=\Gamma\backslash\mathfrak H$.
Adding the $\Gamma$-orbits in $\mathbb P^1(\mathbb Q)$ gives its compactification
$X(\Gamma)$. Write $Y_0(N),Y_1(N),Y(N)$, and similarly $X_0(N),X_1(N),X(N)$.
Their moduli descriptions use elliptic curves with, respectively, a cyclic
subgroup of order $N$, a point of exact order $N$, or a full level-$N$ basis
(with the Weil-pairing convention fixing a connected component). Coarse curves
retain the quotient points; the moduli stack also retains their automorphisms
[@DS05, §§1.5 and 2.1–2.4].

Let $\mu=[\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb Z):\bar\Gamma]$, let $e_2,e_3$ count
elliptic points of orders two and three, and let $c$ count cusps. Then

$$
g(X(\Gamma))=1+\frac{\mu}{12}-\frac{e_2}{4}
-\frac{e_3}{3}-\frac{c}{2}.
$$

Here the index is in the effective projective group; the index in
$\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb Z)$ can differ when $-I\notin\Gamma$.
The formula is Riemann–Hurwitz for $X(\Gamma)\to X(1)$
[@DS05, Theorem 3.1.1].

## Modular forms and Fourier expansions

A *modular form of weight $k$* for $\Gamma$ is a holomorphic function on
$\mathfrak H$ satisfying

$$
f\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)=(c\tau+d)^k f(\tau)
\qquad
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\Gamma,
$$

and holomorphic at every cusp. Its space is denoted $M_k(\Gamma)$.
A *cusp form* vanishes at every cusp; these form $S_k(\Gamma)$.
Holomorphy and vanishing at cusps are conditions on Fourier expansions after
moving the cusp to infinity. If translation by $h$ lies in the resulting
stabilizer, the local parameter is $q_h=e^{2\pi i\tau/h}$; a cusp width cannot
be discarded by writing $q=e^{2\pi i\tau}$ at every cusp. At infinity for
$\Gamma_0(N)$ and $\Gamma_1(N)$ the usual parameter is $q=e^{2\pi i\tau}$.
These spaces are finite dimensional, with dimension formulas obtained from
the geometry of the compact curve [@DS05, §§1.2, 3.5–3.6].

For a Dirichlet character $\chi$ modulo $N$, write $M_k(N,\chi)$ for the
forms with transformation factor $\chi(d)(c\tau+d)^k$ on $\Gamma_0(N)$.
The character must satisfy $\chi(-1)=(-1)^k$ for a nonzero form. The trivial
character gives $M_k(\Gamma_0(N))$.

## Hecke operators and eigenforms

Hecke correspondences induce commuting operators on these spaces. For
$p\nmid N$, their normalized action on $f=\sum_{n\geq0}a_nq^n$ is

$$
T_pf=\sum_{n\geq0}
\bigl(a_{pn}+\chi(p)p^{k-1}a_{n/p}\bigr)q^n,
$$

where $a_{n/p}=0$ if $p\nmid n$. At primes dividing $N$, the operator $U_p$
has expansion $\sum a_{pn}q^n$. The integral Hecke algebra is the
$\mathbb Z$-algebra generated by the Hecke operators and, when appropriate,
diamond operators. A simultaneous eigenform with $a_1=1$ is *normalized*;
then $T_nf=a_nf$ in the standard convention, including the bad-prime
operators. Newforms form a basis of the new subspace. This does not assert
that every old subspace has a basis diagonalizing every bad-prime operator
[@DS05, §§5.2–5.3 and Theorem 5.8.2].

For such an eigenform, the Hecke relations give

$$
a_{mn}=a_ma_n\quad((m,n)=1),\qquad
a_{p^r}=a_pa_{p^{r-1}}-\chi(p)p^{k-1}a_{p^{r-2}}
\quad(r\geq2).
$$

Extend $\chi$ by zero on integers not coprime to $N$. The Dirichlet series
then has Euler product

$$
L(f,s)=\sum_{n\geq1}\frac{a_n}{n^s}
=\prod_p\left(1-a_pp^{-s}+\chi(p)p^{k-1-2s}\right)^{-1}.
$$

At a bad prime the quadratic term vanishes. For a newform these are the
primitive local factors; an oldform or a chosen stabilization requires its
own level and operator conventions [@DS05, Theorem 5.9.2].

Analytic continuation requires hypotheses beyond the existence of a formal
Euler product. For a cusp form the Mellin transform
$\Lambda_N(f,s)=N^{s/2}(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)$ is entire and its
functional equation relates $f$ to its Fricke transform. With the normalized
Fricke convention of [@DS05, §5.10], a Fricke eigenform of sign $\varepsilon$
satisfies $\Lambda_N(f,s)=\varepsilon\Lambda_N(f,k-s)$
[@DS05, Theorem 5.10.2]. Eisenstein series require the corresponding
meromorphic statement.

## Point counts, modularity and isogenies

For an elliptic curve $E/\mathbb Q$ and a prime of good reduction,

$$
a_p(E)=p+1-\#E(\mathbb F_p),\qquad
L_p(E,s)=(1-a_p(E)p^{-s}+p^{1-2s})^{-1}.
$$

The coefficient is the trace of Frobenius, not the point count itself.
At split multiplicative, nonsplit multiplicative, and additive reduction,
the local factors are $(1-p^{-s})^{-1}$, $(1+p^{-s})^{-1}$, and $1$,
respectively. More generally point counts over all finite extensions enter
the zeta function

$$
Z(X/\mathbb F_q,t)=
\exp\left(\sum_{r\geq1}\#X(\mathbb F_{q^r})\frac{t^r}{r}\right).
$$

Thus point counts for a scheme over $\mathbb Z$ lead to local zeta functions;
they are not, without a specified cohomological factor, coefficients of one
modular form [@Sil09, Thm. V.2.2; @DS05, §§8.1 and 8.8].

The modularity theorem of Wiles, Taylor–Wiles, and
Breuil–Conrad–Diamond–Taylor associates to an elliptic curve of conductor $N$
a normalized newform $f\in S_2(\Gamma_0(N))$ with
$L(E,s)=L(f,s)$. Equivalently, $E$ has a nonconstant modular parametrization
$X_0(N)\to E$ over $\mathbb Q$; it is an isogeny factor of $J_0(N)$.
The Eichler–Shimura construction associates an abelian variety $A_f$ to a
weight-two newform, with dimension governed by its coefficient field. For
the rational-coefficient form attached to $E$, this gives an elliptic curve
in its isogeny class [@DS05, §§6.6 and 8.8].

An *isogeny* of elliptic curves is a surjective group morphism with finite
kernel. A finite subgroup determines a quotient $E\to E/K$; for a separable
kernel, Vélu's formulas give explicit equations for this quotient. An isogeny
$\phi:E\to E'$ of degree $d$ has a dual $\widehat\phi:E'\to E$ with
$\widehat\phi\phi=[d]$ and $\phi\widehat\phi=[d]$. Curves isogenous over
$\mathbb Q$ have equal $a_p$ at their good primes. The field of definition
matters in this assertion [@Sil09, Prop. III.4.12, Thm. III.6.1 and Ex. V.5.4].

For the complex torus $E(\mathbb C)=\mathbb C/\Lambda$, with
$\Lambda=\mathbb Z+\mathbb Z\tau$, multiplication by $n$ gives

$$
E[n]=\frac{\frac1n\Lambda}{\Lambda}
\cong(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^2.
$$

Its points are represented by $(a+b\tau)/n$, $0\leq a,b<n$: the points of
$\frac1n\Lambda$ in the half-open fundamental parallelogram. For
$\mathbb C^g/\Lambda$, the same calculation gives
$(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)^{2g}$ and $n^{2g}$ points. This is complex torsion;
the rational torsion subgroup over a number field need not contain these
points [@Sil09, Prop. VI.5.4; @DS05, §1.4].

## Automorphic and computational directions

The adelic formulation places classical modular forms in the automorphic
theory of $\operatorname{GL}_2$ over $\mathbb Q$. It makes the local Hecke
operators and Euler factors part of the same representation-theoretic
description. The reading directions in the original notes are Serre's
*A Course in Arithmetic*, Chapter VII; Shimura's *Introduction to the
Arithmetic Theory of Automorphic Functions*; Bump's *Automorphic Forms and
Representations*; and Stein's *Modular Forms, a Computational Approach*.
Diamond–Shurman supplies the classical route used above, and Silverman
supplies the arithmetic of elliptic curves.

For computation, the original entry points are SageMath's `Gamma0`, `Gamma1`,
`ModularForms`, `CuspForms`, and `ModularSymbols`; elliptic-curve operations
`ap`, `lseries`, `isogeny`, and `torsion_subgroup`; PARI's modular-form
functions `mf*`, together with `ellap` and `lfun`; and the LMFDB for examples.
Modular symbols provide a finite linear-algebra route to Hecke actions.
These are library entry points, not executed computations in this chapter.
The construction problem named in the original notes is to combine this
modular-form and Hecke-algebra structure with finite-index subgroup
generators, retaining the level, character, coefficient ring, and cusp
parameters in every operation.
