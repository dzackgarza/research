Reflective two-elementary lattices

Orientation

Every lattice in the Coble table satisfies \(r + a = 22\): the row indexed by \(n\) has \(r = 10 + n\) and \(a = 12 - n\). The line \(r + a = 22\) is exactly the range in which Alexeev classifies the reflective even hyperbolic \(2\)-elementary lattices and computes their Coxeter diagrams and maximal parabolic subdiagrams [@Ale22], so that classification is a classification of the Coble Picard lattices. This section records it, together with the reflection-theoretic vocabulary it needs, and the consequences for the automorphism groups of the K3 covers.

We work with the sign convention of the Hyperbolic Reflection Groups section: hyperbolic means signature \((1, r-1)\), root lattices are negative definite, and the roots of an even \(2\)-elementary lattice are the short roots \(v^2 = -2\) and the long roots \(v^2 = -4\) with \(\di_L(v) = 2\) ().

Reflective and two-reflective lattices

Reflective and two-reflective

Let \(S\) be an even hyperbolic lattice, and let \(W_2(S)\leq W_r(S)\leq\Orth(S)\) be the subgroups generated by the reflections in the short roots, respectively in all roots, in the notation of . Both are normal in \(\Orth(S)\) and in its index-two subgroup \(\Orth^+(S)\) preserving the positive cone. The lattice \(S\) is two-reflective if \(W_2(S)\) has finite index in \(\Orth(S)\), and reflective if \(W_r(S)\) has finite index in \(\Orth(S)\). Write \(\Gamma_2\) and \(\Gamma_r\) for the Coxeter–Vinberg diagrams () of the two reflection groups, and \(\Gamma_4\subseteq\Gamma_r\) for the subdiagram spanned by the long roots.

Vinberg’s algorithm () terminates on \(S\) for the group \(W_r(S)\) exactly when \(S\) is reflective, and for \(W_2(S)\) exactly when \(S\) is two-reflective; \(\Gamma_r\) is in practice far smaller than \(\Gamma_2\), so it is the diagram to compute [@Ale22 §1]. Reflectivity is a strong restriction: \(a\leq r\) always, and a reflective lattice satisfies \(r\leq 20\) or \(r = 22\), so there are only finitely many reflective \(2\)-elementary lattices [@Ale22 §1].

Coeven and coodd lattices

For a \(2\)-elementary lattice \(S\), the doubled dual is \(S^{\dagger}\da S\dual(2)\), the dual lattice of with its form scaled by \(2\). The lattice \(S\) is coeven if \(S^{\dagger}\) is even and coodd if \(S^{\dagger}\) is odd; these are the cases \(\delta = 0\) and \(\delta = 1\) of the coparity invariant of the Lattice Theory section. A direct sum of \(2\)-elementary lattices is coeven if and only if every summand is.

Duality for coeven lattices

If \(S\) is even hyperbolic \(2\)-elementary and coeven with invariants \((r, a, 0)\), then \(S^{\dagger} = S\dual(2)\) is again even hyperbolic and coeven, with invariants \((r, r - a, 0)\). The Coxeter diagrams \(\Gamma_r(S)\) and \(\Gamma_r(S^{\dagger})\) are dual, in that the short and long roots are interchanged, and this duality induces a bijection between the maximal parabolic subdiagrams of the two, interchanging the odd and even ordinary types of .

Proof. This is [@Ale22 §3].

The decomposition of even hyperbolic two-elementary lattices

Elementary summands

Every even hyperbolic \(2\)-elementary lattice is an orthogonal direct sum of exactly one of the elementary hyperbolic summands \[ \gens{2} = (1,1,1), \qquad U = (2,0,0), \qquad U(2) = (2,2,0) , \] together with a direct sum of negative definite root lattices drawn from the list \[ B_n(2) = (n, n, 1), \quad C_{4n} = (4n, 2, 0)\ (4n\geq 8), \quad C_{4n+2} = (4n+2, 2, 1)\ (4n+2\geq 6), \] \[ F_4 = (4, 2, 0), \qquad E_7 = (7,1,1), \qquad E_8 = (8,0,0), \qquad E_8(2) = (8,8,0) , \] each listed with its invariants \((r, a, \delta)\). Here the non-simply-laced names refer to the full reflection group of the summand; as \((-2)\)-root lattices in the sense of one has \[ B_n(2) = A_1^{\oplus n}, \qquad C_n = D_n, \qquad F_4 = D_4 . \]

The lattices \(D_{2m+1}\) and \(E_6\) do not occur, their discriminant groups being \(\ZZ/4\ZZ\) and \(\ZZ/3\ZZ\), which are not \(2\)-elementary. Main roots and diagrams built on a graph

Main roots

Let \(L\) be an even \(2\)-elementary lattice. The main roots of \(L\) are the short roots \(\alpha\), \(\alpha^2 = -2\), of divisibility \(\di_L(\alpha) = 1\), that is those satisfying \(\beta_L(\alpha, L) = \ZZ\).

The edge \(n\)-fold graph

Let \(G\) be a graph and \(n\geq 2\) an integer. The edge \(n\)-fold graph \(G^{(n)}\) is obtained from \(G\) by subdividing each edge into \(n\) edges, inserting \(n-1\) new vertices on each. Thus \(G^{(n)}\) has \(n\abs{E_G}\) edges and \(\abs{V_G} + (n-1)\abs{E_G}\) vertices, where \(E_G\) and \(V_G\) are the edge and vertex sets of \(G\). The vertices of \(G^{(2)}\) are in natural bijection with the vertices and edges of \(G\) together.

Built on top of a graph

A Coxeter–Vinberg diagram \(\Gamma\) is built on top of a simple graph \(H\) if \(\Gamma\) contains a subdiagram of main roots isomorphic to \(H\) and the remaining roots of \(\Gamma\) are determined by the main roots by explicit rules. For the lattices of the rule is that a main root \(\alpha\) and an additional root \(\beta\) are joined exactly when \(\alpha\cdot\beta = 2\).

Coxeter diagrams on the line \(r + a = 22\)

For \(n = 3, 4, 5, 6\) the Coxeter diagram \(\Gamma_r\) of the even \(2\)-elementary lattice \((10 + n,\, 12 - n,\, 1)\) is built on top of \(K_n^{(2)}\), where \(K_n\) is the complete graph on \(n\) vertices. The diagram of \((18, 4, 0)\) is built on top of \(K_{4,4}^{(2)}\), the complete bipartite graph on \(4 + 4\) vertices, and that of \((14, 8, 0)\) on top of \(D_4^{(2)}\). In every case \[ \Aut\Gamma_r = \Aut G, \qquad \Orth^+(S) = \Aut G \ltimes W_r(S) , \] where \(G\) is the graph in question.

Proof. This is [@Ale22 Thm. 1.4].

The lattices \((10+n, 12-n, 1)\) of are precisely the rows \(n\) of the Coble table, so for \(n = 3, 4, 5, 6\) the Coxeter diagram of the Picard lattice of the K3 cover is built on the doubled complete graph \(K_n^{(2)}\), and the full isometry group of the Picard lattice is the reflection group extended by the symmetric group \(\Aut K_n = S_n\) permuting the \(n\) boundary components.

Non-reflective lattices on the line \(r + a = 22\)

The even \(2\)-elementary lattices \((17, 5, 1)\), \((18, 4, 1)\) and \((19, 3, 1)\) are not reflective. Every other even \(2\)-elementary lattice on the line \(r + a = 22\) is reflective.

Proof. This is [@Ale22 Thm. 1.1].

The three non-reflective lattices are the Picard lattices of the rows \(n = 7, 8, 9\) of the Coble table: \((17,5,1)\) at \(n = 7\), \((18,4,1)\) at \(n = 8\), and \((19,3,1)\) at \(n = 9\). For these three families the reflection group \(W_r\) has infinite index in \(\Orth(S)\), Vinberg’s algorithm does not terminate, and there is no finite Coxeter diagram to fold; the toroidal compactifications built from Coxeter fans are unavailable in exactly these three cases. The remaining eight rows, including the case \(n = 1\) of principal interest, are reflective.

Maximal parabolic subdiagrams and cusps

Maximal parabolic subdiagrams classify isotropic vectors

Let \(S\) be a reflective even hyperbolic lattice, so that Vinberg’s algorithm terminates and produces a finite diagram \(\Gamma_r\). Then the \(\Orth(S)\)-orbits of primitive isotropic vectors of \(S\) correspond bijectively to the maximal parabolic subdiagrams of \(\Gamma_r\), and the orbits modulo \(\Aut\Gamma_r\) to the subdiagrams modulo \(\Aut\Gamma_r\). If a parabolic subdiagram \(\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma_r\) corresponds to \(v\in S\) with \(v^2 = 0\), then the image of \(\Gamma\) in \(v^{\perp}/v\) spans an elliptic root system: of type \(A_n, D_n, E_n\) when every vertex of \(\Gamma\) is a short root, and of type \(A_n(2), D_n(2), E_n(2)\) when every vertex is a long root.

Proof. This is [@Ale22 §1, §2].

Under the two interpretations of an even hyperbolic lattice, this correspondence reads twice. When \(S = S_X\) is the Picard lattice of a K3 surface, its primitive isotropic vectors are the elliptic fibrations of \(X\) (), so the maximal parabolic subdiagrams of \(\Gamma_r(S_X)\) classify the elliptic fibrations up to \(\Orth(S_X)\). When \(S = \delta^{\perp}/\delta\) is the hyperbolic lattice attached to a \(0\)-cusp of a type IV quotient, its isotropic vectors are the \(1\)-cusps adjacent to that \(0\)-cusp, and the corresponding Type II degenerations [@Ale22 §1]; this is the reading used in .

The Coxeter diagram of \(S_\Co\)

The Coxeter diagram \(\Gamma_r\) of \(S_\Co = (11,11,1)_1\) has \(12\) roots and trivial automorphism group \(\Aut\Gamma_r = 1\). It has exactly two maximal parabolic subdiagrams, \[ \widetilde{E}_8(2)\,\widetilde{A}_1 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \widetilde{B}_9(2) , \] both of even ordinary type, so there are two \(\Orth^+(S_\Co)\)-orbits of primitive isotropic vectors in \(S_\Co\).

Proof. This is [@Ale22 §3], the case \((11,11,1)\) of .

What the two orbits do and do not count

Both subdiagrams of have rank \(9 = r - 2\), as a maximal parabolic subdiagram of a rank-\(11\) hyperbolic lattice must, and both are of even ordinary type, so by each associated quotient \(v^{\perp}/v\) has invariants \((9, 9, 1)\). The two orbits are therefore not separated by the invariants of the quotient lattice.

These are orbits of isotropic vectors in the algebraic lattice \(S_\Co\), which by count the elliptic fibrations of the K3 cover of a Coble surface. They are not the \(0\)-cusps of the Coble period space: those are the orbits of primitive isotropic vectors in the transcendental lattice \(T_\Co\) of signature \((2,9)\), of which there is exactly one under the full orthogonal group (). The two lattices share the invariants \((11,11,1)\) but not the signature, and the counts attached to them are different counts.

Reflection groups and automorphisms of the K3 cover

The quotient \(W_r/W_2\)

For an even hyperbolic \(2\)-elementary lattice \(S\) the quotient group \(W_r(S)/W_2(S)\) is isomorphic to the Coxeter group \(W(\Gamma_4)\) generated by the reflections in the long roots.

Proof. This is [@Ale22 §4].

The automorphism group of a two-elementary K3 surface

Let \(S\neq(10,10,0)\) be an even \(2\)-elementary hyperbolic lattice admitting a primitive embedding into \(\lkt\), let \(X\) be a K3 surface with \(S_X = S\), let \(\iota\) be the canonical nonsymplectic involution of \(X\), let \(Y = X/\iota\) with branch divisor \(B\), and let \(\Aut'(Y, B)\) be the subgroup of automorphisms of the pair \((Y, B)\) acting trivially on \(\Pic Y\). Then there is an exact sequence \[ 0 \to \gens{\iota}\times\Aut'(Y, B) \to \Aut(X) \to \operatorname{Sym}\Gamma_r \ltimes W(\Gamma_4) \to 0 , \] where \(\Gamma_4\subseteq\Gamma_r\) is the subdiagram of long roots. In particular \(\Aut(X)\) is finite if and only if \(S_X\) is two-reflective.

Proof. This is [@Ale22 Lem. 4.1]. The proof combines three inputs: the Torelli theorem, by which \(\Aut(X)\to\Orth^+(S)/W_2(S)\) has finite kernel and cokernel and the kernel consists of the automorphisms descending to \((Y, B)\); the surjectivity of \(\Orth(H)\to\Orth(A_H, q_H)\) for indefinite \(2\)-elementary \(H\) (), which lifts an isometry of \(S\) to one of \(\lkt\); and , which identifies \(\Orth^+(S)/W_2(S)\) with \(\operatorname{Sym}\Gamma_r\ltimes W(\Gamma_4)\).

Elliptic fibrations and isotropic vectors

Let \(X\) be a K3 surface with Picard lattice \(S_X\). The primitive isotropic vectors \(v\in S_X\) correspond bijectively to the elliptic fibrations \(X\to\PP^1\), a fibration having \(v\) as the class of its fiber.

Proof. This is [@Huy16 §11.1]; the correspondence in the form used here is [@Ale22 §1].

The ample cone

Ample cone

Let \(X\) be a projective K3 surface with Picard lattice \(S_X\). The ample cone \(A(X)\subseteq S_X\tensor\RR\) is the set of ample classes. It is one connected component of \[ \ts{ x\in S_X\tensor\RR \;\middle|\; x^2 > 0,\ x\cdot d > 0 \text{ for every root } d \text{ of } S_X } , \] that is, one chamber of the complement of the mirrors of the roots inside the positive cone, in the sense of .

The ample cone is a fundamental domain for \(W_2(S_X)\) acting on the positive cone, which is why the Torelli theorem presents \(\Aut(X)\) through the quotient \(\Orth^+(S_X)/W_2(S_X)\): an isometry preserving the ample cone is realized by an automorphism, and every isometry becomes one after composition with a reflection. This is the mechanism in the proof of , and the walls of \(A(X)\) are the mirrors indexed by the vertices of the Coxeter diagram \(\Gamma_2\) of .
