## Basic Theory

::: {.remark}

The study of semitoroidal compactifications of moduli spaces of Coble surfaces largely reduces to lattice theory, of which we will now recall the essential notions.
:::

::: {.remark title="Basic invariants"}

By a **lattice**, we mean a free $\ZZ$-module $L$ of finite rank equipped with a nondegenerate[^lt1] symmetric integral bilinear form $\beta_L: L \tensor_\ZZ L \to \ZZ$.
We abbreviate $vw \definedas \beta_L(v, w)$ and $v^2 \definedas \beta_L(v, v)$ and refer to the latter as the **norm** of $v$.
We write $L_R \definedas L\tensor_{\ZZ} R$ and $\beta_{L_R}$ for $R = \QQ, \RR, \CC$ for the $\ZZ$-linear extensions of $(L, \beta_L)$ to the rational, real, and complex numbers respectively.

A submodule $M\iscontainedin L$ is a **sublattice** if the restricted bilinear form $\restrictionof{\beta_L}{M}$ endows $M$ with the structure of a lattice.
A vector $v\in L$ is **isotropic** if $v^2 = 0$, i.e. it is norm zero, and more generally a sublattice $M \iscontainedin L$ is isotropic if $\restrictionof{\beta_L}{M} \equiv 0$.
A lattice is said to be **even** if $x^2\in 2\ZZ$ for all $x\in L$, and **odd** otherwise.
A nondegenerate symmetric bilinear form can be linearly extended to $L_\RR$ and by Sylvester's theorem, diagonalized with only $1$ or $-1$ on the diagonal.
We write $n_+$ and $n_-$ respectively for the number of $\pm 1$ entries on the diagonal.
The **signature** of $L$ is the pair $(n_+, n_-)$, and the **index** is $n_+ - n_-$.
We say $L$ is **definite** if either $n_+$ or $n_-$ is zero, and **indefinite** otherwise.
More precisely, if $n_- = 0$ we say $L$ is **positive definite**, and similarly if $n_+ = 0$, we say $L$ is **negative definite**. A negative definite lattice generated by elements of norm $-2$ is said to be a **root lattice**, and such elements are referred to as **roots**.

The **rank** $r$ of a lattice is its rank as a free $\ZZ$-module and is given by $r=n_+ + n_- = \dim_\QQ L_\QQ$.
An indefinite lattice of signature $(1, r-1)$ is said to be **hyperbolic**. Fixing a generating set $e_i$ of $L$, we define the **Gram matrix** of $L$ as the matrix $G_L \definedas (\beta_L(e_i, e_j))_{ij}$, and the **discriminant** as $\disc L \definedas \det G_L$.
The discriminant is independent of the choice of generating set.
:::

::: {.remark title="Finiteness in the definite and indefinite cases"}

Write $L[k] \definedas \theset{v\in L \mid v^2 = k}$ for the set of vectors of norm $k$.

If $L$ is definite then $L[k]$ is finite for every $k$, since the real extension of $\pm\beta_L$ is a positive definite quadratic form on $L_\RR$ and $L$ is discrete, so a bounded region contains finitely many lattice points.
For the same reason $\Orth(L)$ is finite: an isometry is determined by the images of a basis $e_1,\dots,e_r$, and it carries $e_i$ into the finite set $L[e_i^2]$.

Neither statement survives indefiniteness.
In $U = \ZZ e\oplus\ZZ f$ one has $U[0] = \ZZ e\union\ZZ f$, which is infinite.
In $L = \generators{1}\oplus\generators{-2}$ the set $L[1]$ is the solution set of the Pell equation $x^2 - 2y^2 = 1$, again infinite, and
$$
M = \begin{bmatrix}3&4\\2&3\end{bmatrix}
\qquad\text{satisfies}\qquad
M^{\mathsf T} G_L M = G_L,
\qquad G_L = \begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&-2\end{bmatrix}
,
$$
with $M$ of infinite order, so $\Orth(L)$ is infinite.
Enumerating a level set or an orthogonal group is therefore not by itself an available operation for the hyperbolic lattices of this monograph, and the reflection groups of the Compactifications sections are described instead by a fundamental chamber with finitely many walls.
:::

## Definite minima, minimal shells, and regularity

::: {.definition #def:definite-minimum-shell title="Minimum, minimal vectors, and kissing number"}

Let $L$ be a definite lattice and put $\varepsilon_L=1$ when $L$ is positive definite and $\varepsilon_L=-1$ when $L$ is negative definite.
The **minimum** of $L$ is
$$
\mu(L)
\definedas
\min_{0\neq x\in L}\varepsilon_L x^2
=
\min_{0\neq x\in L}\abs{x^2}
.
$$
The **minimal shell**, or set of **minimal vectors**, is
$$
\operatorname{Min}(L)
\definedas
\theset{x\in L\sm\theset{0}\st \varepsilon_L x^2=\mu(L)}
,
$$
and the **kissing number** of $L$ is
$$
\tau(L)\definedas\abs{\operatorname{Min}(L)}.
$$
Thus both $x$ and $-x$ are counted.
For a negative-definite lattice these are, by convention, the corresponding invariants of $L(-1)$.
:::

::: {.remark title="Standard terminology and the database convention"}

These are the standard Euclidean-lattice notions; see [@CS10, Ch. 1] and [@Mar03].
The lattice database's `definite.minimum`, `definite.minimal_vectors`, and `definite.kissing_number` use exactly this positive-definite normalization.
A stored list of minimal vectors is required to be the complete shell $\operatorname{Min}(L)$, not merely a generating subset.
:::

::: {.definition #def:voronoi-perfect-lattice title="Voronoi-perfect lattices"}

Let $L$ be definite of rank $r$, and identify $L_\QQ$ with $\QQ^r$ after choosing a basis.
The lattice is **perfect in the sense of Voronoi** if
$$
\spanof_\QQ\theset{vv^{\mathsf T}\st v\in\operatorname{Min}(L)}
=
\operatorname{Sym}_r(\QQ)
.
$$
Equivalently, the values of the quadratic form on its minimal vectors determine the form up to scale.
This is the standard notion of a perfect Euclidean lattice [@Mar03, Ch. 4].
:::

::: {.remark title="Perfect lattice versus perfect bilinear form"}

The word **perfect** is overloaded.
The Voronoi-perfect condition in @def:voronoi-perfect-lattice is a spanning condition on the rank-one tensors of minimal vectors of a definite lattice.
It is unrelated to the categorical use of **perfect bilinear form** for a form whose adjoint $L\to L^\vee$ is an isomorphism.
The database field `definite.perfect` always means Voronoi perfection.
:::

::: {.definition #def:regular-ternary-lattice title="Regular and spinor-regular ternary lattices"}

Let $L$ be a positive-definite integral ternary lattice.
It is **regular** if every positive integer represented by some lattice in the genus of $L$ is represented by $L$ itself.
It is **spinor regular** if every positive integer represented by some lattice in the spinor genus of $L$ is represented by $L$ itself.
For a negative-definite ternary lattice these adjectives refer to $L(-1)$.
:::

::: {.remark}

Regular ternary forms are used in the sense of Jagy--Kaplansky--Schiemann [@JKS97], and spinor regularity in the sense of Earnest--Haensch [@EH19].
Thus `definite.regular` and `definite.spinor_regular` are standard representation-theoretic properties, not database-specific predicates.
The literature states these notions for positive-definite ternary forms; the database's extra convention is only that a negative-definite record is first replaced by $L(-1)$.
:::

::: {.remark title="Discriminant forms"}

The **dual lattice** to $L$ is denoted $\dualof{L} \definedas \Hom_\ZZ(L, \ZZ)$, and there is an morphism
$$
\begin{aligned}
\iota: L &\injects \dualof{L} \\
x &\mapsto \beta_L(x, \cdot)
\end{aligned}
$$
which, if $L$ is nondegenerate, is an injection with finite index image.
The **discriminant group** is $A_L \definedas \coker \iota \cong \dualof{L}/L$; this is a finite order group of order $\abs{\disc L}$.
We say $L$ is **unimodular** if any of the following equivalent conditions hold:

1. $A_L$ is the trivial group,

2. $\iota$ is an isomorphism and $L\cong \dualof{L}$,

3. $\abs{ \disc L} = 1$.

If $A_L \cong (\zpz)^a$ for some $a$, we say $L$ is **$p$-elementary**; in our applications we will often have $p=2$.
For even lattices, the form $\beta_L$ descends to a well-defined quadratic form
$$
\begin{aligned}
q_L: A_L &\to \QQ/2\ZZ \\
x + L &\mapsto \beta_{L_\QQ}(x, x) \mod 2\ZZ
\end{aligned}
$$
We call the pair $(A_L, q_L)$ the **discriminant quadratic form** of $L$.
A **morphism** between two lattices is a morphism of $\ZZ$-modules $\eta: L\to L'$ respecting the bilinear forms in the sense that $\beta_L(x, y) = \beta_{L'}(\eta(x), \eta(y))$, and is a **primitive embedding** if $\eta$ is injective and $\coker \eta$ is torsionfree.
An **isometry** of lattices is an isomorphism, defined in the obvious way.
We write $\Orth(L)$ for the group of lattice automorphisms of $L$, denoted the **orthogonal group** of $L$, and similarly $\Orth(q_L)$ for the $\ZZ$-module automorphisms of the discriminant group $A_L$ which preserve the quadratic form.
There is a natural group homomorphism $\Orth(L)\to\Orth(q_L)$, the kernel is denoted $\tilde \Orth(L)$, equivalently written $\Orth(L)^*$; we call it the **stable orthogonal group**. When $L$ has signature $(2, n)$, the associated period domain $\Omega_L$ has two connected components interchanged by complex conjugation, and we write $\Orth^+(L)$ for the index-two subgroup of $\Orth(L)$ preserving each component (equivalently, preserving the orientation of the positive-definite part, i.e. those isometries of real spinor norm $+1$). We write $\Orth^+(L)^* \definedas \Orth^+(L)\intersect \tilde\Orth(L)$ for the intersection, which is the arithmetic group acting on a single component $D_L$.
:::

## Level, modularity, and the discriminant character

::: {.definition #def:lattice-level title="Arithmetic level"}

Let $L$ be a nondegenerate integral lattice.
Its **level** is the least positive integer $N$ such that
$$
N x^2\in 2\ZZ
\qquad\text{for every }x\in\dualof{L}.
$$
Equivalently, in a basis with Gram matrix $G_L$, the matrix $N G_L^{-1}$ is integral with even diagonal.
This is the standard arithmetic level of an integral lattice [@Han13]; it has no relation to level sets of a function or to level structures on a moduli problem.
The database field `integral.level` stores this standard invariant without changing its definition.
For an even integral lattice it is the level used in the theory of theta series and modular lattices, and it equals the exponent of $A_L$ or twice that exponent [@SSP99].
:::

::: {.definition #def:modular-lattice-global title="$N$-modular lattices"}

Let $L$ be a nondegenerate integral lattice and $N>0$.
Following the modular-lattice literature, $L$ is **$N$-modular** if there is a similarity of the rational quadratic space $L_\QQ$ with multiplier $N$ carrying $\dualof{L}$ onto $L$.
Equivalently,
$$
L\iso\dualof{L}(N)
$$
as lattices.
The case $N=1$ is unimodularity.
:::

::: {.remark title="Relation with the standard definition"}

This is Quebbemann's notion of a modular lattice [@Que95].
The standard terminology is that $L$ is $N$-modular, with $N$ the similarity factor; **modular scale** is the database's name for this stored integer.
The field `integral.modular_scale` stores the multiplier $N$ together with an explicit isometry $L\iso\dualof{L}(N)$.
Quebbemann's original modular-lattice theory is formulated for even lattices; the database uses the same similarity-to-the-dual condition for an arbitrary integral lattice, so the even case is the standard specialization.
The stronger literal equality $N\dualof{L}=L$ inside a fixed rational realization is only a special homothetic case and is not the definition of $N$-modularity.
The modular scale is also distinct from the local scale ideal $\mathfrak{s}(L)$ of the local-invariants section below.
:::

::: {.definition #def:lattice-bad-primes title="The bad-prime set attached to an integral lattice"}

For a nondegenerate integral lattice $L$, set
$$
\Sigma_L
\definedas
\theset{p\text{ prime}\st p\mid 2\disc L}.
$$
This notation is a convention of this monograph and the lattice database.
For an odd prime $p\notin\Sigma_L$, the reduction of $\beta_L$ modulo $p$ is nondegenerate.
If
$$
X_n\definedas\theset{x\in\mathbb{A}^r_\ZZ\st x^{\mathsf T}G_Lx=n},
$$
then $X_n$ is smooth at every prime $p\nmid 2n\disc L$: its gradient is $2G_Lx$, which can vanish modulo such a $p$ only at $x=0$, and $0\notin X_n(\FF_p)$ when $p\nmid n$.
Thus $\Sigma_L$ records the primes at which the lattice form itself can have bad reduction; the fiber $X_n$ has the additional bad primes dividing $n$.
The database field `integral.bad_reduction_primes` stores $\Sigma_L$ in increasing order.
:::

::: {.definition #def:lattice-discriminant-character title="The discriminant quadratic character"}

Suppose $\rank(L)=2m$ and put
$$
D_L\definedas(-1)^m\disc L.
$$
Let $d_L$ be the fundamental discriminant of the quadratic field $\QQ(\sqrt{D_L})$, and set $d_L=1$ when $D_L$ is a square.
The associated quadratic Dirichlet character is
$$
\chi_L(a)
\definedas
\left(\frac{d_L}{a}\right),
$$
the Kronecker symbol.
For every odd $p\notin\Sigma_L$, the nondegenerate quadratic space $L\tensor\FF_p$ is split, i.e. an orthogonal sum of $m$ hyperbolic planes, exactly when $\chi_L(p)=1$.
:::

::: {.remark title="What the database stores"}

The quadratic character itself is standard; the database field `integral.quadratic_character` stores its conductor-discriminant datum $d_L$, not a table of character values.
The split/nonsplit criterion is the usual classification of even-dimensional nondegenerate quadratic spaces over finite fields; compare the local genus theory of [@CS10, Ch. 15].
:::

## Integral hyperbolic index

::: {.definition #def:integral-hyperbolic-index title="Integral hyperbolic index"}

Let $U$ be the unimodular hyperbolic plane.
For a nondegenerate integral lattice $L$, define
$$
h_\ZZ(L)
\definedas
\max\theset{n\geq0\st L\iso U^{\oplus n}\oplus L'\text{ for some lattice }L'}.
$$
We call $h_\ZZ(L)$ the **integral hyperbolic index** of $L$.
This name is a convention of this monograph and the lattice database.
:::

::: {.remark title="Comparison with the Witt index"}

The standard **Witt index** $i_W(L_\QQ)$ is the number of hyperbolic planes split off by the rational quadratic space $L_\QQ$.
The invariant $h_\ZZ(L)$ is an integral refinement:
$$
h_\ZZ(L)\leq i_W(L_\QQ)\leq\min(n_+,n_-),
$$
and equality with the rational Witt index need not hold.
For integral $L$, however, $h_\ZZ(L)$ is also the largest $n$ for which there is an isometric embedding $U^{\oplus n}\injects L$.
Indeed a unimodular sublattice splits orthogonally: if $M\iscontainedin L$ is unimodular, every $x\in L$ has a unique $m\in M$ with $\beta_L(x,\cdot)|_M=\beta_L(m,\cdot)$, so $x-m\in M^{\perp L}$ and $L=M\oplus M^{\perp L}$.
The database field `integral.hyperbolic_index` is $h_\ZZ(L)$, not the rational Witt index.
The rational Witt index is standard quadratic-form terminology [@OM00]; the adjective **integral hyperbolic index** is the project's name for the integral direct-summand refinement above.
:::

::: {.remark title="Group data attached to a lattice"}

For any named subgroup $\Gamma\leq\Orth(L)$, the database field `groups.index_in_orthogonal_group` is the standard group-theoretic index $[\Orth(L):\Gamma]$.
The field `groups.abstract_structure` records the abstract isomorphism type of $\Gamma$ when it is known; it is not an additional invariant of the embedding $\Gamma\injects\Orth(L)$.
Generators, relators, stabilized objects, and orbit representatives record the chosen realization of that abstract group inside $\Orth(L)$.
:::

::: {.definition #def:spinor-norm title="The spinor norm and the special orthogonal group"}

Let $(L,\beta_L)$ be a lattice. Every $g\in\Orth(L_\RR)$ is a product of reflections
$$
g=s_{w_1}\cdots s_{w_m}
$$
in anisotropic vectors $w_i\in L_\RR$, where
$$
s_w(x)=x-2\frac{\beta_L(x,w)}{\beta_L(w,w)}w.
$$
The **real spinor norm** is
$$
\spinornorm_\RR(g)
\definedas
\prod_{i=1}^m\frac{-\beta_L(w_i,w_i)}2
\in
\unitsof{\RR}/(\unitsof{\RR})^2
\cong\theset{\pm1},
$$
independent of the chosen reflection decomposition.
The **special orthogonal group** is
$$
\SO(L)\definedas\ker\qty{\det\colon\Orth(L)\too\theset{\pm1}}.
$$
For a lattice of signature $(2,n)$, $\Orth^+(L)$ denotes the subgroup preserving either connected component of $\Omega_L$; with the convention above this is the trivial-real-spinor-norm subgroup.
:::

::: {.definition #def:k3-moduli-functors title="K3 families, markings, periods, and monodromy"}

Let
$$
\lkt\definedas U^3\oplus E_8^2
$$
with its integral symmetric bilinear form; this is the K3 lattice of signature $(3,19)$.

For a connected complex manifold $S$, let $F_{\Kthree}(S)$ be the groupoid of smooth proper holomorphic maps
$$
f\colon\mathcal X\too S
$$
whose fibers $X_s$ are K3 surfaces, with morphisms the cartesian isomorphisms over $S$.
No polarization, marking, or level structure is part of an object of $F_{\Kthree}$.

For $f\in F_{\Kthree}(S)$, set
$$
\mathbb V_f\definedas R^2f_*\underline{\ZZ}_{\mathcal X}.
$$
Cup product gives a morphism of integral local systems
$$
\beta_f\colon
\mathbb V_f\tensor_{\underline{\ZZ}_S}\mathbb V_f
\too
\underline{\ZZ}_S,
$$
whose fiber at $s\in S$ is the K3 lattice
$$
\qty{H^2(X_s,\ZZ),\smile}.
$$
The weight-two Hodge filtration has
$$
F^2\qty{\mathbb V_f\tensor_\ZZ\mathcal O_S}
=
f_*\Omega^2_{\mathcal X/S},
$$
a line bundle whose fiber is $H^{2,0}(X_s)=\CC\omega_s$.

The **marked K3 functor** $F_{\Kthree}^{\mathrm{marked}}(S)$ has objects $(f,\eta)$ with $f\in F_{\Kthree}(S)$ and
$$
\eta\colon
\qty{\mathbb V_f,\beta_f}
\iso
\qty{\underline{\lkt}_S,\beta_{\lkt})
$$
an isometry of local systems.
Equivalently, $\eta$ is a compatible family of fiber markings
$$
\eta_s\colon
\qty{H^2(X_s,\ZZ),\smile}
\iso
\lkt.
$$
A global marking trivializes the integral local system; monodromy below is therefore defined first for an arbitrary object of $F_{\Kthree}$, using only a marking of one chosen fiber.

For any lattice $L$, write
$$
\Omega_L
\definedas
\theset{
[\omega]\in\PP(L_\CC)
\st
(\omega,\omega)=0,\ (\omega,\bar\omega)>0
}.
$$
If $(f,\eta)\in F_{\Kthree}^{\mathrm{marked}}(S)$, its period map is
$$
\operatorname{per}_{f,\eta}\colon
S\too\Omega_{\lkt},
\qquad
s\longmapsto
[\eta_{s,\CC}(\omega_s)],
$$
where $0\neq\omega_s\in H^{2,0}(X_s)$.
The notation $[\omega]$ always denotes the projective class of a nonzero holomorphic symplectic form after the relevant marking.

Now let $f\in F_{\Kthree}(S)$, choose $s_0\in S$, and choose a marking of the single fiber
$$
\eta_{s_0}\colon
H^2(X_{s_0},\ZZ)\iso\lkt.
$$
The local system $\mathbb V_f$ has monodromy representation
$$
\rho_{f,s_0}\colon
\pi_1(S,s_0)
\too
\Orth\qty{H^2(X_{s_0},\ZZ),\smile}.
$$
Transporting through $\eta_{s_0}$ gives
$$
\rho_{f,s_0}^{\eta_{s_0}}
\definedas
\eta_{s_0}\rho_{f,s_0}\eta_{s_0}^{-1}
\colon
\pi_1(S,s_0)
\too
\Orth(\lkt).
$$
Define the monodromy group of the marked base fiber of the family by
$$
\operatorname{Mon}(f,s_0,\eta_{s_0})
\definedas
\im\rho_{f,s_0}^{\eta_{s_0}}
\iscontainedin
\Orth(\lkt).
$$
Thus monodromy is attached to the local system $\mathbb V_f$; the marking only identifies its fiber with the fixed lattice $\lkt$.

Finally, define
$$
\operatorname{Mon}(\lkt)
$$
to be the subgroup of $\Orth(\lkt)$ generated by the images
$\operatorname{Mon}(f,s_0,\eta_{s_0})$ as $f$, $S$, $s_0$, and the base-fiber marking vary.
:::

::: {.definition #def:k3-positive-cone-orientation title="The orientation character of the K3 lattice"}

Set
$$
\widetilde C_{\lkt}
\definedas
\theset{x\in\lkt_\RR\st(x,x)>0}.
$$
For a positive-definite three-plane $W_3\containedin\lkt_\RR$, the inclusion
$$
W_3\sm\theset{0}\injects\widetilde C_{\lkt}
$$
is a homotopy equivalence: in the orthogonal decomposition
$$
\lkt_\RR=W_3\oplus W_3^\perp,
$$
scaling the $W_3^\perp$-component to zero preserves positivity.
Hence
$$
H_2\qty{W_3\sm\theset{0},\ZZ}
\iso
H_2\qty{\widetilde C_{\lkt},\ZZ}
\iso
\ZZ.
$$
An orientation of $W_3$ determines one of the two generators; these are the **orientation classes** of $\widetilde C_{\lkt}$.
The action on orientation classes defines
$$
\sigma_{\lkt}\colon
\Orth(\lkt_\RR)
\too
\theset{\pm1}.
$$
This is the positive-three-plane form of the real spinor-norm character; set
$$
\Orth^+(\lkt)
\definedas
\ker\sigma_{\lkt}.
$$
This is the convention used for the K3 lattice in Huybrechts, *Lectures on K3 Surfaces*, §5.4, and is the orientation-class formulation used in Markman, *A survey of Torelli and monodromy results for holomorphic-symplectic varieties*, §4.
:::

::: {.proposition #prop:k3-monodromy-positive title="K3 monodromy preserves the positive-three-plane orientation"}

Let $f\in F_{\Kthree}(S)$ with $S$ connected, let $s_0\in S$, and choose a fiber marking
$$
\eta_{s_0}\colon H^2(X_{s_0},\ZZ)\iso\lkt.
$$
Then
$$
\operatorname{Mon}(f,s_0,\eta_{s_0})
\iscontainedin
\Orth^+(\lkt).
$$
Consequently
$$
\operatorname{Mon}(\lkt)
\iscontainedin
\Orth^+(\lkt).
$$
:::

::: {.proof}

For each $s\in S$, choose $0\neq\omega_s\in H^{2,0}(X_s)$ and a Kähler class
$$
H_s\in H^{1,1}(X_s,\RR).
$$
The real three-plane
$$
W_s
\definedas
\Span_\RR\qty{\Re\omega_s,\Im\omega_s,H_s}
\containedin
H^2(X_s,\RR)
$$
is positive definite for the cup-product form.
The ordered basis
$$
\qty{\Re\omega_s,\Im\omega_s,H_s}
$$
defines an orientation class of the positive cone of $H^2(X_s,\RR)$.

This class is independent of the choices: replacing $\omega_s$ by $a\omega_s$ with $a\in\CC^*$ acts orientation-preservingly on the real plane $H^{2,0}(X_s)\oplus H^{0,2}(X_s)$, and the Kähler cone is connected.
In a local trivialization of $\mathbb V_f$, the Hodge line and a Kähler class vary continuously, so these orientation classes form a locally constant section of the two-element orientation local system associated to the positive cones.

Let $\gamma\in\pi_1(S,s_0)$.
Gauss--Manin parallel transport along $\gamma$ therefore preserves the distinguished orientation class at $s_0$.
After applying $\eta_{s_0}$, this says
$$
\sigma_{\lkt}\qty{\rho_{f,s_0}^{\eta_{s_0}}(\gamma)}=+1.
$$
Hence
$$
\im\rho_{f,s_0}^{\eta_{s_0}}
\iscontainedin
\ker\sigma_{\lkt}
=
\Orth^+(\lkt).
$$
Taking the subgroup generated by all such family monodromy images gives the second containment.
:::

::: {.definition #def:degree-2d-k3-period-data title="Degree-$2d$ K3 families and the primitive period lattice"}

Fix a primitive vector
$$
h\in\lkt,
\qquad
h^2=2d,
$$
and set
$$
\lkttd
\definedas
\generators{h}^{\perp\lkt}
\cong
\generators{-2d}\oplus U^2\oplus E_8^2.
$$
Thus $\lkttd$ is the degree-$2d$ primitive K3 lattice already used for $\ftd$.

For a connected complex manifold $S$, an object of $\ftd(S)$ is a pair
$$
\qty{f\colon\mathcal X\too S,\mathcal L}
$$
with $f\in F_{\Kthree}(S)$ and $\mathcal L$ a relatively ample primitive line bundle satisfying
$$
c_1(\mathcal L_s)^2=2d
$$
on every fiber.
The classes $c_1(\mathcal L_s)$ form a flat section
$$
c_1(\mathcal L)\in\Gamma(S,\mathbb V_f),
$$
so the primitive local system
$$
\mathbb V_{f,\mathcal L}^{\mathrm{prim}}
\definedas
c_1(\mathcal L)^\perp
\iscontainedin
\mathbb V_f
$$
is monodromy invariant.

A marking of the polarized family is a marking
$$
\eta\colon\mathbb V_f\iso\underline{\lkt}_S
$$
such that
$$
\eta\qty{c_1(\mathcal L)}=h.
$$
It restricts to
$$
\eta^{\mathrm{prim}}\colon
\mathbb V_{f,\mathcal L}^{\mathrm{prim}}
\iso
\underline{\lkttd}_S.
$$
The marked polarized period map is therefore
$$
\operatorname{per}_{f,\mathcal L,\eta}
\colon
S\too\Omega_{\lkttd},
\qquad
s\longmapsto
[\eta_{s,\CC}(\omega_s)].
$$

For an unmarked polarized family, the monodromy representation of
$\mathbb V_{f,\mathcal L}^{\mathrm{prim}}$ lands, after a base-fiber marking carrying
$c_1(\mathcal L_{s_0})$ to $h$, in
$$
\Orth^+(\lkttd),
$$
equivalently in the subgroup preserving a connected component of the type-IV domain
$\Omega_{\lkttd}$.
This is the polarized form of the positive-orientation statement above.
:::

::: {.proposition #prop:polarized-k3-monodromy-positive title="Polarized K3 monodromy preserves the primitive period component"}

Let $(f\colon\mathcal X\to S,\mathcal L)\in \ftd(S)$ with $S$ connected, choose $s_0\in S$, and choose a fiber marking
$$
\eta_{s_0}\colon H^2(X_{s_0},\ZZ)\iso\lkt
$$
with
$$
\eta_{s_0}\qty{c_1(\mathcal L_{s_0})}=h.
$$
Then the monodromy representation of the primitive local system
$$
\mathbb V_{f,\mathcal L}^{\mathrm{prim}}
$$
lands, after the induced identification with $\lkttd=h^{\perp\lkt}$, in
$$
\Orth^+(\lkttd).
$$
:::

::: {.proof}

Because $c_1(\mathcal L)$ is a flat section of $\mathbb V_f$, every element
$$
g\in\operatorname{Mon}(f,s_0,\eta_{s_0})
\iscontainedin\Orth(\lkt)
$$
fixes $h$.
By @prop:k3-monodromy-positive, $g\in\Orth^+(\lkt)$.
Let
$$
P\containedin\lkttd_\RR
$$
be an oriented positive two-plane.
Since $h^2>0$ and $P\perp h$, the direct sum
$$
\RR h\oplus P\containedin\lkt_\RR
$$
is a positive three-plane.
Orient it by the positive vector $h$ followed by an oriented basis of $P$.
Because $g(h)=h$, preservation by $g$ of the orientation of $\RR h\oplus P$ is equivalent to preservation by $g\vert_{\lkttd}$ of the orientation of $P$.
Hence
$$
g\vert_{\lkttd}\in\Orth^+(\lkttd),
$$
which is equivalently the subgroup preserving a connected component of $\Omega_{\lkttd}$.
:::


::: {.remark title="Orthogonal complements"}

Given two lattices $L_1, L_2$ we write $L_1\oplus L_2$ for the **orthogonal direct sum**, which is the direct sum of the underlying modules with bilinear form defined by
$$
\beta_{L_1\oplus L_2}(v_1 + v_2, w_1 + w_2)\definedas \beta_{L_1}(v_1, w_1) + \beta_{L_2}(v_2, w_2)
.
$$
We write $L^{\oplus n}$ for the direct sum of $n$ copies of $L$.
Let $\eta: M \injects L$ be a primitive embedding of lattices, for example the inclusion of a sublattice.
We write
$$
M^{\perp L} \definedas \theset{x\in L \mid \beta_L(x, M) = 0}
.
$$
If the ambient lattice $L$ is understood, we often abuse notation and simply write $M^{\perp}$ without reference to $L$.
Note that $M^{\perp L}\oplus M \iscontainedin L$ may not be saturated, and is generally a finite index sublattice of $L$.
We note that $M^{\perp L}\intersect M \neq \theset{0}$ in general.[^lt2] We also note that for any lattices $L_i$,
$$
A_{L_1 \oplus \cdots \oplus L_n} = A_{L_1} \oplus \cdots \oplus A_{L_n}
$$
:::

::: {.remark title="2-elementary lattices"}

Let $L$ be a 2-elementary lattice.
The **divisibility** of a vector $v\in L$, denoted $\div_L(v)$, is defined by $\beta_L(v, L) = \div_L(v)\ZZ$, i.e. the positive integral generator of the image of the map $\beta_L(v, \cdot): L\to \ZZ$.
For 2-elementary lattices, one always has $\div_L(v) \in \theset{1, 2}$.
We set $v^* \definedas v/\div_L(v)\in A_L$.
Letting $q_L:A_L \to {1\over 2}\ZZ/\ZZ$ be the induced quadratic form on $A_L$, we say $v^*$ is **characteristic** if $q_L(x) = \beta_L(v^*, x)\mod \ZZ$ for all $x\in A_L$, and is **ordinary** otherwise.
We say that a primitive isotropic vector $e\in L$ is

1. **odd** if $\div_L(e) = 1$,

2. **even ordinary** if $\div_L(e) = 2$ and $e^*$ is ordinary, or

3. **even characteristic** if $\div_L(e) = 2$ and $e^*$ is characteristic.

The 2-elementary hyperbolic lattices admitting a primitive embedding into $\lkt$ were classified by Nikulin in [@Nik80 §3.6.2].
An indefinite **even** 2-elementary lattice is determined up to isometry by its signature together with a triple of invariants $(r,a,\delta)$.
Here, $r\definedas \rank_\ZZ(L)$ is the rank, $a = \rank_{\bF_2}A_L$ is the exponent appearing in $A_L = (\ZZ/2\ZZ)^a$, and $\delta \in \theset{0, 1}$ is the **coparity**: we set $\delta = 0$ if $q_L(A_L) \iscontainedin \ZZ$, so $q_L(x) \equiv 0 \mod \ZZ$ for all $x\in A_L$, and $\delta=1$ otherwise.
The evenness and indefiniteness hypotheses are both needed: the theorem is Nikulin's classification of even 2-elementary lattices, and it rests on the general uniqueness criterion for even indefinite lattices [@Nik80 Thm. 1.14.2].
We accordingly specify such lattices using the notation $(r,a,\delta)_{n_+}$.
:::

::: {.theorem #thm:isotropic-trichotomy title="Classification of primitive isotropic vectors by type"}

Let $S$ be an even hyperbolic 2-elementary lattice with invariants $(r, a, \delta)$, let $v\in S$ be a primitive isotropic vector, and write $\oS\definedas v^{\perp S}/v$.
Then exactly one of the following holds, according to the type of $v$ in the sense above, and in each case $S$ splits off the indicated rank-two summand containing $v$:

1. **odd**: $S \cong U\oplus\oS$, with $a_{\oS} = a$ and $\delta_{\oS} = \delta$;

2. **even ordinary**: $S \cong U(2)\oplus\oS$, with $a_{\oS} = a - 2$ and $\delta_{\oS} = \delta$;

3. **even characteristic**: $S \cong \latI_{1,1}(2)\oplus\oS$, with $a_{\oS} = a - 2$; this case forces $\delta = 1$ and gives $\delta_{\oS} = 0$.

In all three cases $\oS$ is even negative definite of rank $r - 2$.
Classifying the primitive isotropic vectors of $S$ up to isometry is therefore equivalent to classifying the even negative definite lattices with invariants $(r-2, a, \delta)$, $(r-2, a-2, \delta)$ and $(r-2, a-2, 0)$.
:::

::: {.proof}

This is [@AE22 Prop. 5.5].
:::

::: {.remark}

The isotropic-trichotomy theorem (@thm:isotropic-trichotomy) is the tool by which a $0$-cusp of a type IV quotient is identified from a single numerical invariant of its isotropic vector: the divisibility, together with the characteristic-or-ordinary dichotomy, determines the invariants of the boundary lattice $\overline{S}$, and for indefinite $\overline{S}$ those invariants determine its isometry class.
It is used in this form throughout the cusp correspondence.
:::

::: {.remark title="Twists of a lattice"}

If $L$ is a lattice with bilinear form $\beta_L$, define $L(n)$ to be the twist of $L$ by $n$, which has the same underlying $\ZZ$-module but is equipped with the scaled bilinear form
$$
\beta_{L(n)}(v,w) \definedas n\cdot \beta_L(v, w).
$$
:::

::: {.remark title="The lattice $\generators{n}$"}

The lattice $\generators{n}$ is defined as the rank 1 lattice $\ZZ$ with one generator $v$ satisfying $\beta_{\generators{n}}(v,v) = n$.
The Gram matrix is the $1\times 1$ matrix $G_{\generators n} = [n]$, and the associated quadratic form is $q_{\generators{n}}(x) = nx^2$.
:::

::: {.remark title="The hyperbolic lattice"}

In rank 2, there are two unimodular hyperbolic lattices: the odd $\latI_{1, 1} \definedas \generators{1} \oplus \generators{-1}$, and the even $U\definedas \latII_{1, 1}$.
We refer to the latter as the **hyperbolic lattice**, which can be realized as $U \definedas \ZZ e \oplus \ZZ f$ with $e^2=f^2 = 0$ and $ef = 1$, and thus the following Gram matrix:
$$
G_U = \begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
.
$$
:::

::: {.remark title="The lattice $\latI_{p, q}$"}

For any pair of non-negative integers $(p, q)$, there exists an odd indefinite unimodular lattice determined up to isomorphism by its rank and signature:
$$
\latI_{p, q} \definedas \generators{1}^{\oplus p}\oplus \generators{-1}^{\oplus q}
.
$$
:::

::: {.remark title="The lattice $\latII_{p, q}$"}

Let $L$ be an even indefinite unimodular lattice of signature $(p, q)$.
Then $p-q\equiv 0 \pmod 8$, and $L$ is uniquely determined up to isomorphism by its rank and signature:
$$
\latII_{p, q} \definedas
\begin{cases}
    U ^{\oplus p}\oplus E_8^{\oplus {q-p\over 8}}, & p < q \\
    U^{\oplus q} \oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus {p-q\over 8}}, & p > q
.\end{cases}
$$
:::
