# Integral affine geometry {#sec-integral-affine-geometry}

The geometry of a Type III degeneration can be read on a sphere assembled from the anticanonical pairs of its components. Each component supplies a local integral affine model; its double curves prescribe the gluing. The singularities of the resulting affine structure retain the non-toric geometry. We develop the atlas, the local models, and the surgeries before using them for the Coble construction (@sec-ias-degenerations).

## Affine atlases {#sec-affine-atlases}

Let $V$ be a real vector space and $G\to\operatorname{GL}(V)$ a linear action. The affine action associated to it is the semidirect product
$$
G\ltimes V,\qquad (g,v)(h,w)=(gh,v+gw),\qquad x\longmapsto gx+v.
$$
For a full lattice $\Lambda\subset V$, the *integral affine group* is $\operatorname{GL}(\Lambda)\ltimes V$. We distinguish it from the group $\operatorname{GL}(\Lambda)\ltimes\Lambda$, whose transformations also preserve lattice points. An orientation preserving atlas uses $\operatorname{SL}(\Lambda)\ltimes V$. The distinction between real and lattice translations matters: the general integral affine structure has integral linear holonomy, but it has no preferred set of lattice points [@Eng18, Def. 3.1; @AEGS25, §4].

An affine space modeled on $V$ is a nonempty $V$-torsor. A choice of origin identifies it with $V$, but the translation action and difference of points do not require one. A full lattice in $V$ therefore controls linear chart changes without selecting an affine lattice of points.

The same construction works for a specified linear subgroup $G\subseteq\operatorname{GL}(V)$, giving $G\ltimes V$. For a lattice with a quadratic form, its orthogonal specialization is $\operatorname O(L)\ltimes(L\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb R)$; the orthogonal, special linear, and general linear groups express different conditions on the linear part. A $G$-atlas consists of charts whose transition maps are restrictions of elements of the chosen affine group. The group acts on the model space of the charts, not on the manifold before the atlas is given.

Over a coefficient ring $S$, a representation $G(S)\to\operatorname{Aut}_S(L\otimes S)$ similarly gives $G(S)\ltimes(L\otimes S)$. A smaller translation module $W\subseteq L\otimes S$ gives $G(S)\ltimes W$ precisely when the action preserves $W$. For example, $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb Z)$ preserves $\mathbb Z^n$, whereas the full $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb R)$ does not; the expression $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb R)\ltimes\mathbb Z^n$ therefore has no such semidirect-product structure.

::: {.definition #def:integral-affine-atlas title="Integral affine atlas" defines="integral affine atlas; lattice of integral tangent vectors; $\Lambda_B$"}
An *integral affine atlas* on a smooth $n$-manifold $B$ is an atlas $\{\phi_i:U_i\to V\}$ such that, on each connected chart overlap, $\phi_i\phi_j^{-1}$ is the restriction of an element of $\operatorname{GL}(\Lambda)\ltimes V$. Compatible atlases determine the same structure. Its lattice of integral tangent vectors $\Lambda_B\subset TB$ is locally generated by the coordinate vectors of an affine chart.
:::

The transition maps have locally constant linear parts. Thus $\Lambda_B$ is a local system, with linear holonomy $\pi_1(B,b)\to\operatorname{GL}(\Lambda)$. The affine holonomy also records the translation parts; writing either representation as matrices requires a base point and frame, while its conjugacy class does not. An integral affine map has, in charts, the form $x\mapsto Ax+b$ with $A\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda'$; an isomorphism has $A\Lambda=\Lambda'$. These statements concern the selected atlas, not the underlying smooth manifold [@Eng18, Rem. 3.4].

When an application marks *integral points*, it must specify a compatible subset whose chart images lie in $\Lambda$. This is additional data, and its coordinate changes use lattice translations. A lattice of tangent vectors exists even when no such point set has been selected.

::: {.definition #def:singular-integral-affine-surface title="Singular integral affine surface" defines="singular integral affine surface; essential point; integral affine sphere"}
A *singular integral affine surface* is a topological surface $B$, a finite subset $\Sigma\subset B$, and an integral affine atlas (@def:integral-affine-atlas) on $B\setminus\Sigma$. A point of $\Sigma$ is essential when this atlas does not extend across it. An *integral affine sphere* has $B\simeq S^2$.
:::

The linear holonomy around a puncture detects many essential singularities. A chosen integral triangulation, polarization, or involution is further structure; none is determined by the topological sphere. In particular, the dual complex of a degeneration determines incidence, while the integral affine atlas is supplied by the anticanonical data [@Eng18, Prop. 3.10; @AEGS25, Thm. 4.4].

## Lagrangian torus fibrations {#sec-lagrangian-affine}

A regular Lagrangian fibration $\pi:(M^{2n},\omega)\to B^n$ has Lagrangian fibers. In action-angle coordinates near a compact connected regular fiber, $\pi$ is the projection of $U\times T^n\to U$, and the period lattice in $T^*B$ gives the base its integral affine structure. The regular fibers are $n$-tori. For an almost toric fibration in dimension four, elliptic singularities occur over the boundary and focus-focus fibers over isolated interior values. Around a simple focus-focus value, the linear monodromy is conjugate to the shear $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ [@Sym03; @Eng18, Rem. 4.1]. A branch cut displays the atlas; it is not an additional singular fiber.

Conversely, an integral affine manifold $B$ has a regular Lagrangian torus fibration. Let $\Lambda_B^*\subset T^*B$ be the lattice locally generated by the closed forms $dx_1,\ldots,dx_n$ of an integral affine chart. Translation by these forms preserves the canonical symplectic form on $T^*B$, so it descends to $T^*B/\Lambda_B^*$. The projection $T^*B/\Lambda_B^*\to B$ has Lagrangian torus fibers. This construction does not require a global choice of affine coordinates.

In local Darboux coordinates $(x_1,y_1,x_2,y_2)$, the four almost toric models are the regular projection $(x_1,x_2)$, the corank-one elliptic map $(x_1,x_2^2+y_2^2)$, the corank-two elliptic map $(x_1^2+y_1^2,x_2^2+y_2^2)$, and the focus-focus map $(x_1y_1+x_2y_2,x_1y_2-x_2y_1)$ [@Sym03]. On a compact almost toric base, a regular interior fiber is $T^2$; a fiber over the interior of a regular boundary edge is $S^1$; a toric vertex has a point fiber; and a focus-focus value has a pinched torus fiber. The dashed branch cut in a base diagram records a choice of affine chart across its two sides.

## Anticanonical pairs and charge {#sec-ias-charge}

Let $(V,D)$ be an anticanonical pair with $D$ a cycle of rational curves $D_1+\cdots+D_r$, and put $d_i=-D_i^2$. Its *charge* is $Q(V,D)=\chi_{\mathrm{top}}(V\setminus D)$. For a cycle with at least two components,
$$
Q(V,D)=12+\sum_{i=1}^{r}(d_i-3).
$$
For an irreducible nodal $D$, the formula is $Q(V,D)=11-D^2$; for smooth $D$, it is $12\chi(\mathcal O_V)-D^2$ [@AE23, Def. 3.7; @Eng18, Def. 2.6]. We use the first formula only with its stated hypothesis.

::: {.definition #def:ias-pseudofan title="Pseudofan" defines="pseudofan; $\mathcal F(V,D)$"}
The *pseudofan* $\mathcal F(V,D)$ is the cone (@def:closed-cone) on the dual cycle of $D$, triangulated by its cone edges. The radial edge associated with $D_i$ has negative self-intersection $d_i=-D_i^2$ when $r>1$, and $d_i=2-D_i^2$ when $r=1$. These weights determine the integral affine structure away from the cone point [@Eng18, Def. 3.8].
:::

The atlas extends over the cone point precisely for a toric pair [@Eng18, Prop. 3.9]. Thus a non-toric pair contributes a singular local model. Corner blowups of the pair subdivide its pseudofan without changing charge. A corner blowup equivalence class records this choice of toric subdivision; it does not identify every pair with the same boundary cycle [@Eng18, Def. 3.8 and Props. 3.9–3.13].

::: {.definition #def:corner-blowup-class title="Corner blowup equivalence" defines="corner blowup equivalent; corner blowup equivalence class; cbec"}
Two pseudofans (@def:ias-pseudofan) are *corner blowup equivalent* when finite sequences of corner blowups and their inverses, with each inverse contracting a boundary $(-1)$-curve, connect them. The source (@def:globular-boundary) notes call an equivalence (@def:equivalence-of-objects) class a *corner blowup equivalence class* (*cbec*). It retains the singular affine germ at the cone (@def:closed-cone) point while changing its triangulated presentation [@Eng18, §3].
:::

## Type III spheres {#sec-type-iii-ias}

For a one-parameter K3 degeneration, Type III is the maximal
degeneration: the logarithm $N$ of unipotent monodromy on $H^2$ has
$N^2\ne0$, and the dual complex of a Kulikov model is two-dimensional.
This is the K3 instance of a *large complex structure limit*. More
generally, the essential skeleton of a Calabi–Yau degeneration is a
model-independent invariant; for a minimal dlt model it equals that
model's skeleton. Maximality means that this skeleton has dimension
equal to the dimension of the Calabi–Yau fiber [@NX13; @KS06]. A
polarization records an additional class in the family, and its
existence alone does not establish maximal degeneration.

For a Type III Kulikov central fiber $X_0=\bigcup_i V_i$, the double locus $D_i\subset V_i$ is an anticanonical cycle, and the dual complex $\Gamma(X_0)$ triangulates $S^2$. The pseudofan of $(V_i,D_i)$ is the star of the vertex representing $V_i$. Gluing these stars across the edges of $\Gamma(X_0)$ gives the integral affine atlas away from its singular vertices [@AE23, Prop. 3.3; @Eng18, Prop. 3.10]. For a double curve of arithmetic genus $g$, the double-curve relation is
$$
(D_{ij})^2+(D_{ji})^2=-2+2g.
$$
For a smooth rational double curve, $g=0$ and the sum is $-2$; the arithmetic-genus formula retains the nodal case as well.
The additional $d$-semistability condition is needed for smoothing the assembled surface to a K3 surface [@AE23, Thm. 3.5]. These conditions belong to the central fiber; the topology of the dual complex alone does not imply them.

Conversely, start with anticanonical pairs $(V_i,D_i)$ and identifications $D_{ij}\simeq D_{ji}$ that match the triple points. The pushout along these double curves gives a normal crossings central fiber. If its dual complex is $S^2$, its double curves obey the relation above, and the fiber is $d$-semistable, Friedman's smoothing theorem applies [@AE23, Prop. 3.3 and Thm. 3.5]. Ordered toric models and choices of origins on the double curves can make this construction uniform in a family [@AE23, Def. 7.4 and Constr. 7.5; @AEGS25, §4].

A triangulated integral affine sphere must retain the corner blowup
equivalence class of an anticanonical pair at each singularity
(@sec-ias-charge). With this local data and a triangulation by unit lattice
triangles, the reverse construction interprets the star of each vertex as
an anticanonical pair and glues its boundary curves. For fixed combinatorial
data, the gluing periods form the torus
$\operatorname{Hom}(\Lambda,\CC^*)\cong(\CC^*)^{19}$
[@AE22, Construction 6.12]. Thus the affine sphere does not specify a
single algebraic central fiber; the gluing periods remain part of the data.

The conservation of charge reads
$$
\sum_i Q(V_i,D_i)=24
$$
for a Kulikov surface [@AE23, Prop. 3.8]. This counts charge, rather than singular points: one singularity can have charge greater than one. When every singularity is of type $I_1$, there are twenty-four nodes. A chosen integral triangulation of the sphere is retained separately from its affine structure.

If the singularities are all of type $I_n$, an integral affine sphere of
total charge $24$ also determines a Lagrangian torus fibration on a
symplectic four-manifold diffeomorphic to a K3 surface. Its singular fibers
are necklaces of spheres, with $n$ components over an $I_n$ point
[@AE22, §6D]. This symplectic construction is distinct from choosing the
complex gluing periods above. In the Enriques application one must further
make the affine involutions, triangulation, and polarization compatible;
the symmetric parameters are described in @sec-ias-degenerations.

## Boundary self-intersections {#sec-ias-edge-intersections}

Let $P$ be an integral affine disk with boundary edges $P_i$. Near an edge choose an oriented lattice basis $(x_i,y_i)$ with $x_i$ tangent to the next edge and $y_i$ tangent to $P_i$. If $y_{i-1}$ is transported into this chart, its *negative boundary self-intersection* $d_i$ is characterized by
$$
d_i y_i=y_{i-1}-x_i.
$$
If a common chart contains the next edge as well, this becomes $d_i y_i=y_{i-1}+y_{i+1}$. The first equation is the local definition; the second needs the additional chart. For the base of an almost toric fibration of $(V,D)$, $d_i=-D_i^2$ [@Eng18, Def. 4.2]. A single boundary component requires the corresponding chart on the universal cover of its neighborhood.

## Surgeries {#sec-integral-affine-surgeries}

The three changes to a boundary cycle have different effects on charge. Here $d_i=-D_i^2$ and the displayed formulas apply to cycles with at least two components [@Eng18, Remark 2.7 and Prop. 4.5; @AE23, Defs. 3.7–3.9].

| Operation | Boundary cycle | Change in $Q$ |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| Corner blowup | $(d_i,d_{i+1})\mapsto(d_i+1,1,d_{i+1}+1)$ | $0$ |
| Internal blowup | $d_i\mapsto d_i+1$ | $1$ |
| Node smoothing | $(d_i,d_{i+1})\mapsto d_i+d_{i+1}-2$ | $1$ |

A *corner blowup* is the blowup of a node $D_i\cap D_{i+1}$, with the exceptional curve inserted into the anticanonical boundary. In a toric chart it cuts a basis triangle from the polygon corner and inserts the edge of weight $1$. The two adjacent weights increase by one. The new term $(1-3)$ cancels the two increments in the charge formula [@Eng18, Remark 2.7; @AE23, Def. 3.9].

An *internal blowup* is the blowup of a smooth point of $D_i$; its exceptional curve is not part of the strict transform of the boundary. In a regular affine chart of a disk $P$, take a triangle $T$ whose base is a proper subsegment of $P_i$, whose remaining points lie in the regular interior, and which is an integer multiple $n$ of a basis triangle. Delete $T$ and identify its two exposed edges by the affine shear that fixes the interior vertex and preserves the line of $P_i$. The interior vertex becomes an $I_1$ singularity; the two pieces of $P_i$ remain one boundary edge. The size $n$ records the removed triangle, while a single internal blowup raises the edge weight and charge by one [@Eng18, Def. 4.3 and Prop. 4.5].

A *node smoothing*, or nodal trade, starts at a vertex $v=P_i\cap P_{i+1}$. For a positive integer $n$, cut along the segment from $v$ to $v+n(x_i+y_i)$ in the regular interior. Identify its two sides by the shear that fixes the cut pointwise and sends $x_i$ to $-y_i$. The endpoint in the interior becomes an $I_1$ singularity; $P_i$ and $P_{i+1}$ straighten into one edge. With the first basis vector directed from that singularity toward $v$, the shear has matrix $\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ [@Eng18, Def. 4.4]. This is a change of affine charts around the cut, not an identification of the whole disks as integral affine spaces.

A *nodal slide* moves a focus-focus value along its monodromy eigenline while retaining the affine structure off the path. The cut changes length, and the boundary cycle and charge remain fixed. For the corresponding almost toric fibrations, nodal slides and trades yield symplectomorphic total spaces under the hypotheses of the almost toric construction [@Sym03; @Eng18, Rem. 4.1].

### The triangle of $\mathbb P^2$ {#sec-ias-p2-example}

For $D=C_1+C_2+C_3$ the toric boundary of $\mathbb P^2$, each line has $C_i^2=1$, so $(d_1,d_2,d_3)=(-1,-1,-1)$ and $Q=12+3(-4)=0$. The source drawing uses a tenfold dilation of the standard moment triangle; this scale leaves its primitive edge directions and boundary weights unchanged. Its polygon vertices $p_{ij}$ correspond to the toric points $q_{ij}=C_i\cap C_j$. Blowing up the three toric vertices in turn gives
$$
(-1,-1,-1)\longmapsto(0,1,0,-1)
\longmapsto(0,1,1,1,0)
\longmapsto(1,1,1,1,1,1).
$$
Each cycle has charge zero. The final boundary consists of six $(-1)$-curves. The source diagrams of these three steps remain in the [native TikZ figure sources](#sec-ias-native-figures).

## Gluing and triangulation {#sec-ias-gluing}

A real $C^k$ manifold is Hausdorff and second countable, with finite-dimensional charts and transition maps of class $C^k$; smooth manifolds use $k=\infty$. A complex $m$-manifold has holomorphic charts and yields a smooth real manifold of dimension $2m$. A surface has pure real dimension two, independently of compactness, orientation, or boundary. Polygon pieces have boundary or corners before gluing, so their local models must admit those strata [@Lee13, Chs. 1–2].

A realized convex polytope retains its placement in an affine space as well as its abstract face incidence. A lattice polytope additionally has a selected affine lattice; a Euclidean metric is separate data. Spherical and hyperbolic geodesic polytopes have their own ambient geometry, but their incidence complexes still admit faces, stars, and links. A triangulation $T$ of a polytope $P$ includes a realization $|T|\to P$ and face inclusions. To call it an *integral triangulation*, choose affine lattice-point markings on the charts, compatible under their transition maps, and require its vertices to lie in these marked sets. Such markings are extra data beyond an integral tangent lattice. A *unimodular triangulation* has normalized lattice volume one in every maximal simplex. These conditions do not choose the triangulation [@Zie95, Chs. 1–2; @Hat02, §2.1].

A family of polygonal cells with identified boundary faces first determines a quotient cell complex. An affine atlas on its regular part requires the face identifications to extend across collars by integral affine maps. Around a vertex, the cyclic composite of these maps determines the holonomy; a nonextendible chart marks an affine singularity. A triangulation includes its cells, attaching maps, and realization onto the quotient. Integrality of its vertices is relative to the compatible lattice-point markings just specified. These are distinct choices: the face poset alone has no affine holonomy [@Eng18, Prop. 3.7; @AEGS25, §4].

More precisely, a face gluing specifies a finite diagram of cells and face maps. Its quotient comes with a map from every original cell. A surgery selects a subregion $Q\subset P$, a replacement $Q'$, and an identification of their boundaries; its space is the pushout of $P\setminus\operatorname{int}Q$ and $Q'$ along that identification. The local surface condition and extension of affine charts are then checked on this particular quotient. Neither follows from the abstract colimit alone.

For a regular triangulated surface, the link of an interior vertex is a circle and the link of a boundary vertex is an interval. Face identifications can instead produce a nonregular cell complex, where a face poset alone loses attaching data [@Hat02, §2.1]. An automorphism of that incidence complex need not preserve the affine atlas. Likewise, an involution on the completed affine sphere is a map of that structured sphere; a symmetry visible in a planar drawing supplies no such map without an equivariant realization [@AEGS25, Prop. 4.5].

::: {.question #qst:cellular-attaching-diagrams title="What category of cell shapes encodes CW attaching maps?"}

Can one choose a category of shapes so that its diagrams encode cellular attaching maps and realization recovers the glued space? Consider $X_d=S^1\cup_{f_d}D^2$, where $f_d(z)=z^d$ on the boundary circle. For $d=1,2$ the cells and their face incidences agree. In the cellular chain complex the boundary from degree two to degree one is multiplication by $d$, so $H_1(X_1;\ZZ)=0$ and $H_1(X_2;\ZZ)=\ZZ/2$. Repeating a cell once for each element of an indexing set gives copies of the cell. The degree is carried by the map from its boundary [@Hat02, §2.2].

Which morphisms in the proposed shape category record that map, repeated face incidences and their compositions? For a dual complex obtained from a group action, which identifications record the action of a cell stabilizer on its boundary? For an integral-affine realization, which maps also transport the lattice charts across faces? These are distinct requirements on diagrams that have the same face poset.
:::

For a simplex $\sigma$ in $T$, its *closed star* is the subcomplex of simplices that lie in simplices containing $\sigma$. Its *link* consists of simplices disjoint from $\sigma$ whose join with $\sigma$ lies in $T$. A subdivision carries a realization map back to the original complex. An incidence automorphism, an integral affine automorphism, and an isometry of a chosen metric preserve successively different data; one does not obtain the last two from a permutation of faces alone.

A regular CW complex has embedded closed cells. A simplicial complex records simplices meeting in common faces; a $\Delta$-complex permits more face identifications. A simplicial set also has degeneracy maps, and its Kan condition is an additional horn-filling property. These presentations do not follow merely from calling a quotient a dual complex [@Hat02, §2.1]. Two commuting involutions determine an action of $C_2\times C_2$ when they are specified as maps of the completed affine sphere. A planar unfolding records edge pairings; an embedding of the completed sphere requires an ambient space of dimension at least three.

In the degree-two K3 construction @cons:degree-two-k3-affine-sphere, gluing the Symington disk $P(\lambda)$ to its oppositely oriented copy along the common boundary gives the topological sphere $B(\lambda)$. Its affine charts across the equator and its singularities are part of the construction, and a polarization or an involution supplies still more data [@AET23, Construction 6.1]. The Coble and Enriques construction (@sec-ias-degenerations) uses related affine spheres with their marked roots and cusps.
