# Toric geometry from cones and polytopes {#sec-toric-geometry}

A toric variety can be built from monomials on affine charts, then glued along the charts of faces. Its fan records the gluing, while a polytope records an ample divisor. These two descriptions are useful when a component of a K3 degeneration has a toric model and when an integral affine polygon describes that component's boundary.

## Affine semigroup charts

All cones in a fan below are rational and strongly convex. This ensures that each affine chart has dense torus $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[M]$.

Let $N\simeq\mathbb Z^n$ be a lattice, $M=\operatorname{Hom}(N,\mathbb Z)$ its dual, and $\langle\ ,\ \rangle:M\times N\to\mathbb Z$ the pairing. For a rational polyhedral cone $\sigma\subset N_{\mathbb R}$, put
$$
\sigma^\vee=\{m\in M_{\mathbb R}:\langle m,u\rangle\geq0\text{ for all }u\in\sigma\},
\qquad S_\sigma=\sigma^\vee\cap M,
\qquad U_\sigma=\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[S_\sigma].
$$
Gordan's lemma makes $S_\sigma$ finitely generated. The monomial $\chi^m$ corresponds to $m\in S_\sigma$, with $\chi^m\chi^{m'}=\chi^{m+m'}$ [@Ful93, §1; @CLS11, Ch. 1]. A face $\tau=\sigma\cap m^\perp$, with $m\in S_\sigma$, gives the open immersion $U_\tau\simeq (U_\sigma)_{\chi^m}\hookrightarrow U_\sigma$. For a fan $\Sigma$, glue its $U_\sigma$ along these face opens. The result $X_\Sigma$ is a normal toric variety, and its dense torus is $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[M]$ [@Ful93, §1].

A cone is *strongly convex* when $\sigma\cap(-\sigma)=\{0\}$. This condition makes $\sigma^\vee$ full dimensional. A cone is *simplicial* when its rays are linearly independent. It is *regular* when its primitive ray generators extend to a basis of $N$; the affine toric chart is smooth exactly in this case. Thus full dimension of the dual tests strong convexity, while regularity tests smoothness [@CLS11, §§1.2, 3.1].

For a full-dimensional two-dimensional cone $\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}(v_1,v_2)$, the rows of the inverse of the matrix with columns $v_1,v_2$ give real generators of $\sigma^\vee$ up to positive scale. One must clear denominators and divide each generator to its primitive lattice vector. For example,
$$
\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}((0,1),(2,-1)),\qquad
\sigma^\vee=\operatorname{Cone}((1,2),(1,0)).
$$
For $\sigma=\operatorname{Cone}((0,1),(2,-1))$, the Hilbert basis is $(1,0),(1,1),(1,2)$. Hence
$$
\mathbb C[S_\sigma]\simeq\mathbb C[x,y,z]/(y^2-xz).
$$
The surface has an $A_1$ singularity. The dual rays alone do not generate this semigroup: the interior lattice point $(1,1)$ is essential. In higher dimension the semigroup generators must likewise be found in $\sigma^\vee\cap M$, rather than read only from its extremal rays.

The zero cone gives the torus $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[M]$. If $\sigma$ is generated by the first $r$ members of a basis of $N$, then $U_\sigma\simeq\mathbb A^r\times(\mathbb G_m)^{n-r}$. The open torus of $\mathbb A^1$ is $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[t,t^{-1}]$. The formal disc and its punctured generic fiber instead use $\operatorname{Spf}\mathbb C[[t]]$ and $\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C((t))$; these are formal analogues of the affine line and its open torus, with different local geometry.

The three-dimensional cone
$$
\operatorname{Cone}(e_1,e_2,e_3,e_1+e_2-e_3)
$$
has dual semigroup generated by $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$, $(1,0,1)$, and $(0,1,1)$. Their one relation gives the affine quadric threefold
$$
U_\sigma\simeq\operatorname{Spec}\mathbb C[x,y,z,w]/(xw-yz).
$$
This cone is not simplicial: it has four extremal rays in dimension three. By contrast, every strongly convex two-dimensional cone is simplicial, though it need not be regular; the resulting affine toric surface singularity is a cyclic quotient singularity [@CLS11, §§1.3, 3.1].

## Normalization and finite maps

A morphism $f:Y\to X$ is *finite* when each affine open $\operatorname{Spec}A\subset X$ has affine inverse image $\operatorname{Spec}B$ with $B$ a finite $A$-module. Having finite fibers is necessary but insufficient: the open immersion $\mathbb G_m\hookrightarrow\mathbb A^1$ has fibers of size at most one, yet is not finite. For an irreducible affine variety $X=\operatorname{Spec}A$, its normalization is $\operatorname{Spec}\overline A\to X$, where $\overline A$ is the integral closure of $A$ in its function field. Over $\mathbb C$ this morphism is finite and birational [@CLS11, §1.3].

For an affine semigroup $S$, the normalization of $\mathbb C[S]$ is $\mathbb C[\overline S]$, where $\overline S=\operatorname{Cone}(S)\cap\operatorname{gp}(S)$ is its saturation. The semigroup $S_\sigma=\sigma^\vee\cap M$ is already saturated, which explains the normality of the toric charts above. The $A_1$ ring $\mathbb C[x,y,z]/(y^2-xz)$ is normal despite being singular: normalization and resolution perform different operations [@CLS11, §1.3].

## Fans, divisors, and blowups

The torus-invariant prime divisors of a normal toric variety correspond to rays $\rho\in\Sigma(1)$. If $u_\rho$ is the primitive generator, the invariant Weil divisor $D=\sum_\rho a_\rho D_\rho$ has associated polyhedron
$$
P_D=\{m\in M_{\mathbb R}:\langle m,u_\rho\rangle\geq-a_\rho\text{ for all }\rho\}.
$$
Its lattice points index a basis $\{\chi^m:m\in P_D\cap M\}$ of $H^0(X_\Sigma,\mathcal O(D))$ when $D$ is Cartier. A compatible integral linear function on each cone is the Cartier condition. Set $\psi_D(u_\rho)=-a_\rho$. For a complete fan, $D$ is basepoint free exactly when $\psi_D$ is the minimum of its linear pieces, and ample exactly when this minimum is strictly piecewise linear across every wall. This is concavity in the usual analytic convention; toric references call it *upper convexity*. Thus arbitrary facet coefficients do not by themselves make a divisor Cartier or ample [@CLS11, §§4.2, 6.1].

For a smooth complete $n$-dimensional toric variety, the divisor sequence gives
$$
0\longrightarrow M\longrightarrow\mathbb Z^{\Sigma(1)}
\longrightarrow\operatorname{Pic}(X_\Sigma)\longrightarrow0,
\qquad \operatorname{rank}\operatorname{Pic}(X_\Sigma)=|\Sigma(1)|-n.
$$
For a full-dimensional polytope with this normal fan, its rays correspond to the *facets* of the polytope. The number of vertices need not equal the number of rays [@Ful93, §3; @CLS11, §4.2].

A subdivision of a fan gives a proper toric birational morphism. In a smooth surface cone $\operatorname{Cone}(u_1,u_2)$, inserting the ray through $u_1+u_2$ is the ordinary blowup of the torus-fixed point. Inserting the primitive ray through a general $a u_1+b u_2$ instead gives a toric weighted blowup, possibly with quotient singularities; it is not generally the ordinary blowup of an ideal written $(x^a,y^b)$ [@CLS11, §3.3]. A complete fan is polytopal exactly when it admits a strictly concave integral piecewise-linear support function. Completeness alone does not ensure projectivity in dimensions at least three [@CLS11, §6.1].

For a smooth projective toric variety with a torus-invariant Kähler form whose cohomology class is integral, the moment-map image is a polytope in $M_{\mathbb R}$; after fixing the moment-map translation and prequantum line bundle, this is the polytope of the associated ample line bundle. Its normal fan recovers the toric variety. This gives the symplectic reading of the same polytope used in the integral affine construction (@sec-lagrangian-affine) [@Ful93, §4; @CLS11, §12.3].

## Polytopes and polar duality

Let $P\subset M_{\mathbb R}$ be a full-dimensional lattice polytope. Its *inward* normal cone at a face $F$ consists of $u\in N_{\mathbb R}$ for which $\langle m,u\rangle$ is minimized on $F$. These cones form the complete normal fan $\Sigma_P$. When $0$ lies in the interior, the normal fan is also the fan of cones over faces of the polar $P^\circ$ defined below. The construction $P\mapsto(X_{\Sigma_P},L_P)$ produces a projective toric variety with an ample line bundle. Conversely, an ample invariant Cartier divisor determines a lattice polytope up to translation [@Ful93, §§2–3; @CLS11, Ch. 6].

The *normalized lattice volume* is $\operatorname{Vol}_M(P)=n!\operatorname{vol}_{\mathrm{Eucl}}(P)$, where a fundamental lattice parallelepiped has Euclidean volume $1$. The intersection number is $L_P^n=\operatorname{Vol}_M(P)$ [@Ful93, §5]. For $\mathbb P^2$, the standard simplex $\Delta_2=\operatorname{Conv}(0,e_1,e_2)$ has normalized area $1$ and gives $\mathcal O(1)$. Its dilation $3\Delta_2$ has normalized area $9$ and gives $\mathcal O(3)=-K_{\mathbb P^2}$.

Assume $0\in\operatorname{int}(P)$. Our polar convention is
$$
P^\circ=\{u\in N_{\mathbb R}:\langle m,u\rangle\geq-1
\text{ for every }m\in P\}.
$$
The polytope $P$ is *reflexive* if $P^\circ$ is a lattice polytope. Equivalently, every facet has equation $\langle m,u_F\rangle=-1$ for a primitive inward normal $u_F\in N$, and $P^\circ=\operatorname{Conv}\{u_F\}$. A face $F$ of dimension $i$ corresponds to a face of $P^\circ$ of dimension $n-1-i$. The origin is the unique interior lattice point of a reflexive polytope [@CLS11, §8.3].

The anticanonical divisor on $X_\Sigma$ is $-K_X=\sum_{\rho\in\Sigma(1)}D_\rho$. Therefore $X$ is Gorenstein Fano exactly when its anticanonical polytope $P_{-K_X}$ is reflexive and $\Sigma$ is its normal fan [@CLS11, Thm. 8.3.4]. Smoothness is a separate condition: every maximal cone of $\Sigma$ must be regular. There are sixteen reflexive polygons up to lattice equivalence, and five give smooth toric del Pezzo surfaces: $\mathbb P^2$, $\mathbb P^1\times\mathbb P^1$, and blowups of $\mathbb P^2$ at one, two, or three torus-fixed points [@CLS11, §8.3].

For $\mathbb P^n$, the anticanonical polytope is $(n+1)\Delta_n-\sum_{i=1}^n e_i$. Its normal fan gives $\mathbb P^n$. In dimension four, a general anticanonical hypersurface is a quintic Calabi–Yau threefold. In dimension two, the polytope is the triangle $3\Delta_2-e_1-e_2$, with polar $\operatorname{Conv}(e_1,e_2,-e_1-e_2)$ after a suitable identification of the dual lattice. For a smooth degree-$d$ hypersurface $D\subset\mathbb P^n$, adjunction gives $K_D=(d-n-1)H|_D$; hence $-K_D$ is ample when $d<n+1$, while $d=n+1$ gives trivial canonical bundle. For $n\geq2$ this is the Calabi–Yau degree.

A normal toric variety is *Gorenstein* exactly when its canonical divisor $K_X$ is Cartier. A Fano variety has ample $-K_X$. A smooth Fano curve is $\mathbb P^1$. In dimension three there are eighteen smooth toric Fano varieties up to isomorphism, as classified by [Batyrev](https://www.mathnet.ru/php/archive.phtml?jrnid=im&option_lang=eng&paperid=1581&wshow=paper). The Mori program studies the contractions and flips detected by $K_X$ and its intersections with curves; in the toric setting, these operations can be read from changes to the fan [@CLS11, Ch. 15].

## Toric models of anticanonical pairs

A Type III Kulikov component is an anticanonical pair $(V,D)$: $K_V+D\sim0$. A toric model is a span
$$
(V,D)\longleftarrow(V',D')\longrightarrow(\bar V,\bar D),
$$
with corner blowups on the left and interior blowups from a toric boundary on the right (@def:toric-model-anticanonical). A corner blowup at a boundary node has $K_{V'}=\pi^*K_V+E$ and reduced boundary $D'=\pi^*D-E$, since the strict transform is $\pi^*D-2E$ and the exceptional curve joins the cycle. An interior blowup at a smooth boundary point has the same canonical formula and strict boundary $D'=\pi^*D-E$. In both cases $K_{V'}+D'=\pi^*(K_V+D)$. The interior blowup adds one unit of charge; the corner blowup preserves it (@prop:charge-under-blowup). These operations supply the local pieces of the integral affine sphere in @sec-integral-affine-geometry.

A regular Lagrangian fiber of the corresponding almost toric fibration is a two-torus; a simple focus-focus fiber is a pinched torus (@sec-lagrangian-affine). On the mirror side, the dual complex of a Type III Kulikov fiber is an integral affine sphere of total charge $24$ (@sec-type-iii-ias). Thus the fan and polytope calculations here lead to the anticanonical components used in the stable Enriques construction, while the affine sphere also records their global gluing data.
