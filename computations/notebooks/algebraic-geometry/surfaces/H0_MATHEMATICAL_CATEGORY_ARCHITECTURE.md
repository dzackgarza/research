# Mathematical category architecture for the \(H^0(\mathcal O(4,4))^\tau\) program

## 0. Scope and conventions

This document specifies the mathematical language needed by the research in
`H0_O_P1xP1_4_4.ipynb`.  It answers four questions.

1. Which objects, morphisms, categories with axioms, replete full
   subcategories, groupoids, groups, modules, and posets are needed?
2. Which implications and preservation results connect them?
3. Which operations belong in Sage `ParentMethods`, `ElementMethods`,
   `MorphismMethods`, and homset parents?
4. On which mathematically restricted categories can coordinate algorithms
   provide those operations?

Unless another base is stated, rings are commutative with identity and ring
homomorphisms preserve identity.  The immediate research field is
\(k=\mathbf Q\), but the general language records every use of perfectness,
algebraic closure, characteristic \(0\), or invertibility of a group order.

For a scheme \(X\), \(|X|\) denotes its topological space.  A point
\(x\in|X|\), a \(K\)-rational point
\(\operatorname{Spec}K\to X\), and a geometric point
\(\operatorname{Spec}\Omega\to X\) are different objects.  The phrase
"geometric singular point" means a point after base change to an algebraic
closure.

The word "node" means a geometric ordinary double point.  For a
one-dimensional scheme locally of finite type over a field, this is the
condition in [Stacks, Nodal curves](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0C46):
after base change to an algebraic closure, the completed local ring is
\[
\overline{k}[[u,v]]/(uv).
\]
For a hypersurface curve germ on a regular surface in characteristic different
from \(2\), a nondegenerate quadratic initial form gives this geometric
condition.

## 1. Sage categories as mathematical structure

### 1.1 The four constructions

The architecture uses four different constructions.  They must not be
collapsed into one inheritance tree.

#### Category with an axiom

If a property is invariant under isomorphism and leaves the homsets unchanged,
it defines a replete full subcategory.  In Sage this is the role of
`CategoryWithAxiom` and a method in `SubcategoryMethods`.

Examples include:

- reduced commutative rings;
- Noetherian commutative rings;
- reduced schemes;
- normal schemes;
- smooth objects of `Schemes(S)`, where smoothness refers to the structure
  morphism.

If \(C\) is a category and `Reduced` is defined on \(C\), a subcategory
\(D\subseteq C\) obtains `D.Reduced()` through `_with_axiom("Reduced")`.  The
parent, element, and morphism classes generated by Sage then receive methods
from every applicable category with an axiom.  This is the mechanism that
should make operations appear dynamically.

The dotted category expressions in this document are semantic notation.
Existing Sage factories such as `IntegralDomains()`, `Fields()`, and
`NoetherianRings()` remain authoritative.  A dotted expression is realized
by an existing factory, a category created by `CategoryWithAxiom`, or a
category join according to Sage's current category graph; it must not shadow
a native category with a second definition.

#### Join of categories imposing independent axioms

Several independent hypotheses are expressed by a join in Sage's lattice of
categories.  A curve over \(k\) is not a Python subclass unrelated to schemes;
it is the named join
\[
\mathsf{Curves}(k)
=
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{FiniteType}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{Separated}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{PureDimension}(1).
\]

Likewise,
\[
\mathsf{SmoothProjectiveCurves}(k)
=
\mathsf{Curves}(k)
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{Smooth}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{Projective}.
\]

The name abbreviates the join; it does not duplicate its methods.

#### Categories of additional data and forgetful functors

Additional data or a stricter notion of morphism gives a new category and a
projection or forgetful functor, not a replete full subcategory with the same
homsets.

| Category | Objects | Morphisms | Projection |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| \(\mathsf{CAlg}_R\) | \(R\to A\) | commuting triangles | to \(\mathsf{CRing}\) |
| \(\mathsf{Schemes}(S)\) | \(X\to S\) | commuting triangles over \(S\) | to \(\mathsf{Schemes}\) |
| \(\mathsf{LocalRings}\) | local rings | local homomorphisms | to \(\mathsf{CRing}\) |
| \(\mathsf{PointedSchemes}(S)\) | \((X,x)\) over \(S\) | maps carrying \(x\) to \(y\) | to \(\mathsf{Schemes}(S)\) |
| \(\mathsf{EmbeddedSchemes}\) | closed immersions \(X\hookrightarrow P\) | commutative squares | to an arrow category |
| \(\mathsf{Actions}(G,C)\) | functors \(BG\to C\) | natural transformations | evaluation to \(C\) |
| \(\mathsf{PolarizedSchemes}(S)\) | proper finitely presented \(X\to S\) with relatively ample \(L\) | maps with compatible pullback isomorphisms | to \(\mathsf{Schemes}(S)\) |
| \(\mathsf{CyclicCoverData}_n\) | \((Y,L,s)\) | pullback-compatible isomorphisms | to \(\mathsf{Schemes}\) |

The first six projections in the table are faithful because an arrow is
determined by its underlying arrow.  The last two need not be faithful:
distinct chosen invertible-module isomorphisms can lie over one scheme
morphism.  Method reuse follows only the data actually forgotten.  A local
ring delegates ordinary ring operations to its underlying commutative ring,
but `LocalRings()` does not claim that every ring homomorphism between two
local rings is local.

#### Full subcategories indexed by mathematical parameters

Properties with a parameter give indexed families of full subcategories
rather than Boolean axioms:

- `PureDimension(d)`;
- `RelativeDimension(d)`;
- `FiniteLocallyFree(rank=r)`;
- plane curve germs geometrically of type \(A_n\).

The parameter is mathematical data and remains visible in the notation for
the full subcategory.  In Sage, a category constructor may build the member
of this indexed family.

A \(G\)-grading is additional structure, not an intrinsic property of an
ungraded ring or module.  Thus `GradedCommutativeAlgebras(R,G)` and
`GradedModules(A,G)` are categories of graded objects indexed by \(G\), with
forgetful functors to the corresponding ungraded categories.

### 1.2 Placement and recognition

A constructor returns its output in the most specific categories established
by the mathematics available at that call.  There are exactly three grounds
for placing a result in a category with an axiom or another replete full
subcategory.

1. **Construction theorem.**  A cited theorem applies to the constructor and
   states that its output has the defining property.  Prime localization, for
   example, returns a local ring because
   \(R_{\mathfrak p}\) has unique maximal ideal
   \(\mathfrak pR_{\mathfrak p}\).
2. **Computation of the predicate.**  On a category where the predicate is
   decidable, the relevant computation returns `True`.  For example, a
   radical computation for a finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebra may
   show that the defining ideal is radical, after which the quotient is
   placed in reduced rings.
3. **Categorical implication.**  An established implication among categories
   applies.  A domain is reduced; a regular Noetherian scheme is normal; a
   smooth morphism is flat and locally of finite presentation.

If none of these applies, the output remains in the strongest supercategory
whose defining properties have been established.  If the construction
retains genuine mathematical data, it may instead lie in the category of
that data, with a projection to its underlying objects.  Extra data means a
map, section, ideal, action, embedding, grading, or other mathematical datum,
not a statement about why a property should hold.

There are no proof, certificate, evidence, or trust objects in this
architecture.  A citation is documentation for the construction theorem; it
is not runtime data.  A computation exposes the computed ideal, map,
dimension, factorization, or predicate value; it does not emit prose that
claims to prove the result.  No `Proof`, `Certificate`, `Evidence`,
`LocalRingCertificate`, or software-encoded proof burden appears in a
constructor or category.

The resulting rule is:

\[
\text{construct or compute}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{place in the corresponding subcategory when justified};
\qquad
\text{otherwise retain the established category}.
\]

| Construction | Resulting placement |
| --- | --- |
| \(R/I\), \(I\) radical | reduced commutative rings |
| \(R/\mathfrak p\), \(\mathfrak p\) prime | integral domains |
| \(R/\mathfrak m\), \(\mathfrak m\) maximal | fields |
| \(S^{-1}R\), \(R\) reduced | reduced commutative rings |
| \(R_{\mathfrak p}\) | `CommutativeRings().Local()`, hence an object of `LocalRings()`, with maximal ideal \(\mathfrak pR_{\mathfrak p}\) |
| \(\operatorname{Spec}R\), \(R\) reduced | reduced affine schemes |
| \(X_{\mathrm{red}}\) | reduced schemes |
| an open subscheme of a normal scheme | normal schemes |
| a base change of a smooth morphism | smooth scheme morphisms |
| pullback of an invertible module | invertible modules |
| fixed scheme of an automorphism of a separated scheme | closed subschemes |
| relative spectrum of cyclic-cover algebra | finite locally free morphisms of rank \(n\) |

An author may place a canonical parent directly in a category with an axiom
when a standard theorem establishes the property.  On such a parent the
corresponding predicate follows from category membership, and the source is
cited in its mathematical documentation.

Recognition from arbitrary input is a separate matter.  For example:

- a finitely generated \(\mathbf Q\)-algebra may compute its
  nilradical and enter the category with the `Reduced` axiom;
- a finite-dimensional \(\mathbf Q\)-algebra may compute all of
  its maximal ideals from an explicit finite presentation supplied to the
  computation, and enters the full subcategory of local objects exactly when
  that set has one element;
- a finitely presented local \(\mathbf Q\)-algebra may compare
  dimension and embedding dimension and enter the category with the
  `Regular` axiom;
- an arbitrary abstract commutative ring does not acquire a generic procedure
  for deciding whether it is local.

The visible surface consists of the full subcategories containing the result
and the rings, ideals, maps, sections, embeddings, or chosen equations used
by the construction.

### 1.3 Homsets and morphism methods

For every category \(C\) and objects \(X,Y\in C\), the object
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_C(X,Y)
\]
is a Sage parent and its elements are the morphisms.  The homset parent owns:

- domain and codomain;
- the category of morphisms;
- construction of morphisms from data appropriate to \(C\);
- the identity when \(X=Y\);
- composition with compatible homsets.

`MorphismMethods` owns operations on a morphism:

- `domain()`, `codomain()`, and `parent()`;
- composition; `End(X)` supplies the identity morphism, while deciding whether
  another endomorphism equals it belongs only to homsets with decidable
  equality;
- universal factorizations supplied by the mathematical construction;
- base change for arrows in a category with pullbacks, restriction for sheaf
  morphisms, and the induced maps specified by each universal construction.

A functor between categories indexed by a morphism belongs to that indexing
morphism, not to the internal morphisms of either fixed-source or
fixed-target category.  Thus a ring map owns extension of scalars between
module categories, and a scheme map owns pullback between
quasi-coherent-module categories.  The fact that a convenient call may be
spelled on an object, such as `M.base_change(alpha)`, does not change this
mathematical ownership.

Universal constructions return ordinary objects and ordinary morphisms.
For example:

- a quotient returns \((R/I,q:R\to R/I)\);
- a localization returns \((S^{-1}R,\ell:R\to S^{-1}R)\);
- a fiber product returns \(X\times_S Y\) and its two projections;
- an equalizer returns the equalizer object and its monomorphism.

If \(f:R\to A\) kills \(I\), the quotient morphism supplies the unique map
\(\bar f:R/I\to A\).  If \(f\) sends \(S\) to units, the localization morphism
supplies the unique map \(S^{-1}R\to A\).  These are methods on the relevant
morphisms or homsets, not wrappers around them.

The Sage references governing this division are:

- [the category primer](https://doc.sagemath.org/html/en/reference/categories/sage/categories/primer.html);
- [categories with axioms](https://doc.sagemath.org/html/en/reference/categories/sage/categories/category_with_axiom.html);
- [category-generated classes](https://doc.sagemath.org/html/en/reference/categories/sage/categories/category.html);
- [homsets](https://doc.sagemath.org/html/en/reference/categories/sage/categories/homsets.html).

## 3. Categorical substrate

### 3.1 Categories to reuse or supply

The common substrate consists of:

- `Sets()`;
- existing Sage algebraic categories such as `Rings()`,
  `CommutativeRings()`, `Fields()`, `Modules(R)`, and
  `CommutativeAlgebras(R)`;
- `Homsets(C)`;
- `Arrows(C)`, whose objects are morphisms of \(C\) and whose morphisms are
  commutative squares;
- `Slice(C,S)` and `Coslice(C,R)`;
- finite diagram categories \([I,C]\);
- functor categories needed for group actions;
- products, fiber products, equalizers, coequalizers, kernels, cokernels, and
  pullbacks on the categories where they exist.

`CommutativeAlgebras(R)` is the coslice
\((R\downarrow\mathsf{CRing})\).
`Schemes(S)` is the slice
\((\mathsf{Schemes}\downarrow S)\).
These identifications should control their homsets and structure maps.

### 3.2 Method ownership

| Surface | Mathematical responsibility |
| --- | --- |
| category or factory methods | construction of zero objects, products, joins, homsets, and other operations whose input is the category rather than one object |
| category `ParentMethods` | operations valid for every parent in the category |
| category `ElementMethods` | operations valid for every element of such a parent |
| category `MorphismMethods` | operations valid for every morphism in the category |
| nested `Homsets.ParentMethods` | operations on homset parents |
| forgetful functor from a category of additional data | reuse of operations from the target category |
| axiom category | stronger operations and implications justified by that axiom |
| set, module, group, or poset over a fixed object | ideals, sections, divisors, or constructible subsets |

Sage's generated classes should do the method assembly.  Concrete
mathematical parents supply their data and category; they do not reproduce
the inherited method list.

For sheaves and sheaves of modules, the element parent must be stated.  A
sheaf \(\mathcal F\) does not have one set containing all of its local
sections.  For every open \(U\subseteq X\), it supplies a parent
\(\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)\), and for every \(x\in U\) it supplies a germ map
\(\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)\to\mathcal F_x\).  Thus restrictions and germs are
methods on elements of `Sections(U,F)`, not generic `ElementMethods` on an
unspecified sheaf object.

### 3.3 Sheaves, ringed spaces, and relative affine constructions

These notions precede schemes.  They are not utilities attached to a scheme
implementation.

For a topological space \(X\), use the categories

\[
\mathsf{Sh}(X),\qquad
\mathsf{ShRing}(X),\qquad
\mathsf{Mod}(\mathcal O_X).
\]

The first is the category of sheaves of sets on \(X\), the second the
category of sheaves of commutative rings, and the third the category of
sheaves of \(\mathcal O_X\)-modules for a fixed sheaf of rings
\(\mathcal O_X\).  Restriction, sheafification, stalks, kernels, cokernels,
tensor products, and internal Hom belong here.  In particular:

- sheafification is the left adjoint from presheaves to sheaves;
- the stalk functor at \(x\in X\) is the filtered colimit over
  neighborhoods of \(x\);
- two morphisms of sheaves are equal exactly when their maps on every stalk
  are equal, and a morphism of sheaves is an isomorphism exactly when every
  stalk map is an isomorphism;
- a sheaf of modules is finite locally free of rank \(r\) when it is locally
  isomorphic to \(\mathcal O_X^{\oplus r}\).

An object of `RingedSpaces()` is a pair
\((X,\mathcal O_X)\).  A morphism

\[
(f,f^\#):(X,\mathcal O_X)\longrightarrow(Y,\mathcal O_Y)
\]

consists of a continuous map \(f:X\to Y\) and a morphism
\(f^\#:\mathcal O_Y\to f_*\mathcal O_X\), equivalently
\(f^{-1}\mathcal O_Y\to\mathcal O_X\).  The category
`LocallyRingedSpaces()` has locally ringed spaces as objects and only those
ringed-space morphisms for which

\[
\mathcal O_{Y,f(x)}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{X,x}
\]

is local for every \(x\in X\).  Thus it has a faithful, non-full forgetful
functor to `RingedSpaces()`: locality restricts the morphisms, not merely the
objects.

`Schemes()` is the full subcategory of `LocallyRingedSpaces()` consisting of
objects admitting an open cover by locally ringed spaces isomorphic to
\(\operatorname{Spec}(A)\).  This definition fixes the direction of every
map on functions and the source of the local-ring condition on scheme
morphisms.

For a morphism of ringed spaces \(f:X\to Y\), sheaf operations are functorial:

\[
f_*:\mathsf{Mod}(\mathcal O_X)\longrightarrow
\mathsf{Mod}(\mathcal O_Y),
\qquad
f^*\mathcal G
=
\mathcal O_X\otimes_{f^{-1}\mathcal O_Y}f^{-1}\mathcal G.
\]

When \(f\) is a scheme morphism, `QuasiCoherentModules(X)` is the full
subcategory of \(\mathsf{Mod}(\mathcal O_X)\) formed by quasi-coherent
modules, and pullback restricts to

\[
f^*:\mathsf{QCoh}(Y)\longrightarrow\mathsf{QCoh}(X).
\]

Pushforward does not preserve quasi-coherence without hypotheses.  If \(f\)
is quasi-compact and quasi-separated, then \(f_*\) carries
\(\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\) to \(\mathsf{QCoh}(Y)\); this restriction belongs to the
join of those two morphism subcategories, not to every scheme morphism.

For a scheme \(S\), `QuasiCoherentAlgebras(S)` is the category of
commutative algebra objects in \(\mathsf{QCoh}(S)\).  Relative spectrum is an
equivalence

\[
\underline{\operatorname{Spec}}_S:
\mathsf{QCAlg}(S)^{\mathrm{op}}
\simeq
\mathsf{AffineSchemes}(S).
\]

It sends \(\mathcal A\) to an affine morphism
\(\underline{\operatorname{Spec}}_S(\mathcal A)\to S\), commutes with base
change, and recovers
\(\mathcal A\) as the direct image of the structure sheaf.  Relative spectrum
is an object method on a quasi-coherent algebra, and an algebra morphism
induces the opposite affine-scheme morphism.  A base morphism indexes the
base-change comparison.  None of these is a special case of
polynomial-ring code.

Likewise, a quasi-coherent graded \(\mathcal O_S\)-algebra
\(\mathcal A=\bigoplus_{d\geq0}\mathcal A_d\) defines
\(\underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_S(\mathcal A)\).  A chosen finite set of
homogeneous generators
or a chosen closed immersion into projective space is additional
presentation data; it is not part of the intrinsic object
\(\underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_S(\mathcal A)\).

The corresponding method ownership is:

| Category | `ParentMethods` | `ElementMethods` | `MorphismMethods` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `SheavesOfSets(X)` | section parents, restrictions, and stalks | none until a section parent is chosen | maps on sections and stalks |
| `Sections(U,F)` | the set, group, ring, or module operations appropriate to \(\mathcal F\) | restriction to smaller opens, germ at \(x\in U\), and image under a sheaf morphism | restriction maps and maps induced by sheaf morphisms |
| `SheavesOfRings(X)` | module category and section-ring parents | none until a section ring is chosen | restriction of scalars |
| `Modules(OX)` | section-module parents, tensor, internal Hom, kernels, cokernels | none until a section module is chosen | induced maps on stalks |
| `RingedSpaces()` | underlying space and structure sheaf | no universal element model; points belong to the underlying topological space | direct image, inverse image, and module pullback |
| `LocallyRingedSpaces()` | local rings and residue fields as methods taking a point \(x\in|X|\) | no universal element model | local maps on stalks |
| `QuasiCoherentAlgebras(S)` | relative spectrum and section-algebra parents | none until a section parent is chosen | induced morphisms of relative spectra |

The definitions and functorial directions are fixed by
[morphisms of ringed spaces](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0094),
[locally ringed spaces](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01HA),
[schemes](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01II), and
[relative spectrum](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01LQ), together with
[functoriality for quasi-coherent modules](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01LA).

## 4. Commutative algebra

### 4.1 Ring categories and categories with axioms

The foundational algebraic categories are:

```text
CommutativeRings()
  .Noetherian()
  .Artinian()
  .Jacobson()
  .Nagata()
  .GRing()
  .J2()
  .UniversallyCatenary()
  .QuasiExcellent()
  .Excellent()
  .Reduced()
  .IntegralDomain()
  .Normal()
  .Regular()
  .CohenMacaulay()
  .Gorenstein()

CommutativeAlgebras(R)
  .FiniteType()
  .FinitePresentation()
  .GeometricallyReduced()       # R a field
  .GeometricallyIntegral()      # R a field
  .GeometricallyNormal()        # R a field
```

Here `Regular`, `CohenMacaulay`, and `Gorenstein` use their Noetherian
definitions, so these categories imply `Noetherian`.  A normal ring means a
reduced ring whose localizations at prime ideals are integrally closed
domains; the intersection `Normal().IntegralDomain()` is the category of
normal domains.  This convention permits products of normal domains while
keeping `NormalDomains()` as the integral specialization.

The finiteness categories used by normalization must also be explicit:

- a Jacobson ring is a ring in which every radical ideal is the intersection
  of the maximal ideals containing it;
- a Nagata ring is a Noetherian ring \(R\) such that
  \(R/\mathfrak p\) is Japanese for every prime
  \(\mathfrak p\subset R\), where a domain is Japanese when its integral
  closure in every finite extension of its fraction field is finite over it;
- a Noetherian ring is a G-ring when, for every prime
  \(\mathfrak p\), the completion map
  \(R_{\mathfrak p}\to\widehat{R_{\mathfrak p}}\) is regular, equivalently its
  formal fibers are geometrically regular;
- a Noetherian ring satisfies \(J\)-2 when every finite-type \(R\)-algebra has
  open regular locus;
- a Noetherian ring is universally catenary when every finite-type
  \(R\)-algebra is catenary;
- a quasi-excellent ring is a Noetherian G-ring satisfying \(J\)-2;
- an excellent ring is quasi-excellent and universally catenary.

These are categories with axioms because they change which general theorems,
and hence which methods, apply.  They are not implementation labels.

The implication graph includes:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{Field}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Artinian},\mathsf{Noetherian},\mathsf{Regular},
\mathsf{Normal},\mathsf{Reduced};\\
\mathsf{IntegralDomain}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Reduced};\\
\mathsf{Regular}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Normal}
\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Reduced};\\
\mathsf{Gorenstein}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{CohenMacaulay};\\
\mathsf{Artinian}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Noetherian};\\
\mathsf{Excellent}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{QuasiExcellent}\cap\mathsf{UniversallyCatenary};\\
\mathsf{QuasiExcellent}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Nagata}
\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Noetherian}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Geometrically integral implies geometrically reduced and geometrically
irreducible.  Geometrically normal implies normal and geometrically reduced.
A finite-type algebra over a field is excellent, Nagata, and Jacobson.

### 4.2 Ring `ParentMethods`

Methods valid on every commutative ring:

- `Hom(S)` and `End()`;
- `ideal(generators)`, `zero_ideal()`, and `unit_ideal()`;
- quotient by an ideal, returning the quotient map;
- localization at a multiplicative system, returning the localization map;
- localization at a prime ideal, returning a local ring;
- finite products;
- spectrum;
- the prime spectrum \(\operatorname{Spec}R\) and its subset
  \(\operatorname{MaxSpec}R\) of maximal ideals;
- module category over the ring.

Methods on restricted categories:

| Category | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| integral domains | fraction field; valuation-related constructions on smaller categories with the required valuation hypotheses |
| Noetherian rings | completion along a finitely generated ideal as an adic construction |
| finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras | radical, nilradical, reduction, associated primes, primary decomposition, dimension, and elimination |
| reduced rings with finitely many minimal primes | integral closure in the product of the fraction fields of the irreducible components, with the normalization map |
| reduced Nagata rings | finite normalization |
| reduced finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras | computed normalization, presented by generators and relations together with the finite normalization map |
| pure-dimensional finite-type \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras of dimension \(d\) | the singular subscheme of \(\operatorname{Spec}A\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf Q\), cut out by \(\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{A/\mathbf Q})\); its underlying set is both the nonsmooth and nonregular locus |
| Artinian rings | the canonical finite product decomposition \(R\cong\prod_{\mathfrak m\in\operatorname{MaxSpec}R}R_{\mathfrak m}\) |
| finite-dimensional \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras with a finite presentation | computed maximal ideals, Chinese-remainder idempotents, and explicit presentations of the local Artinian factors |
| regular local rings | dimension equals embedding dimension, and every minimal generating set of the maximal ideal is a regular system of parameters |
| regular finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras | smoothness of \(\operatorname{Spec}A\to\operatorname{Spec}\mathbf Q\) |

`is_reduced()`, `is_domain()`, `is_normal()`, and similar predicates return
`True` from subcategory membership.  On a larger category they exist only
when a genuine decision procedure is total on that mathematical category.
An explicit generators-and-relations presentation is input to such a
computation; it does not define a second category containing the intrinsic
predicate.

The intrinsic normalization construction and an algorithm for presenting it
must not be conflated.  The former exists in the stated mathematical
generality.  Finiteness follows in the category with the `Nagata` axiom.
The executable normalization method in the present program is restricted to
reduced finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras and returns a finite
generators-and-relations presentation together with the normalization map.

### 4.3 Ring `ElementMethods`

General ring elements support:

- their principal ideal;
- annihilator as an ideal.

For a finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebra, exact ideal membership decides
whether \((a)=R\), and computed associated primes decide whether \(a\) is a
zero divisor.  The resulting element is then recognized in `Units(R)` or in
the distinguished subset of zero divisors.  Fields and domains inherit the
corresponding conclusions directly from their categories.

The corresponding categories with axioms, indexed full subcategories, and
distinguished subsets add:

- valuation and order methods on discrete valuation rings;
- nilpotence index on the distinguished subset of nilpotent elements in a
  finite-dimensional \(\mathbf Q\)-algebra;
- degree on homogeneous elements of a graded ring, while an arbitrary
  element has a finite homogeneous support when the grading is a direct sum.

### 4.4 Ring `MorphismMethods`

For \(f:R\to S\):

- evaluation \(x\mapsto f(x)\);
- kernel as an ideal and image as a subring;
- finite generators-and-relations presentations of those subobjects on
  homsets of finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras with an
  elimination procedure;
- induced maps on quotients when \(f(I)\subseteq J\);
- induced maps on localizations when the chosen multiplicative system maps to
  units;
- extension and contraction of ideals;
- maps on spectra in the opposite direction;
- base change of \(R\)-algebras;
- maps on modules of differentials.

For a quotient map \(q:R\to R/I\), the factorization operation accepts
\(f:R\to A\) with \(I\subseteq\ker f\) and returns the unique map
\(\bar f:R/I\to A\).  For a localization map, the corresponding operation
accepts a map sending the localized elements to units.

### 4.5 Ideals and distinguished subposets

For each commutative ring \(R\), `Ideals(R)` is a parent.  Its elements are
ideals, not new ring categories.  The poset
\(\operatorname{Idl}(R)\), ordered by inclusion, has distinguished subsets
and subposets:

- `PrimeIdeals(R)`;
- `MaximalIdeals(R)`;
- `RadicalIdeals(R)`;
- `PrimaryIdeals(R)`;
- homogeneous ideals of a graded ring.

Ideal operations include:

- sum, intersection, product, quotient, radical, and saturation;
- extension and contraction;
- localization;
- associated primes and primary components under Noetherian hypotheses;
- Fitting ideals of finitely presented modules;
- closed subschemes through the spectrum functor.

These subsets control the category of a quotient.  Quotient by a radical
ideal is reduced, quotient by a prime ideal is a domain, and quotient by a
maximal ideal is a field.

### 4.6 \(R\)-algebras

`CommutativeAlgebras(R)` has objects \(R\to A\) and morphisms commuting with
the structure maps.

`ParentMethods`:

- `base_ring()` and `structure_map()`;
- tensor product over \(R\);
- module of Kahler differentials \(\Omega_{A/R}\);
- symmetric, Rees, and graded constructions when their module input is
  supplied;
- relative spectrum.

`MorphismMethods`:

- underlying ring morphism;
- compatibility with structure maps;
- induced map on differentials;
- relative spectrum morphism in the opposite direction.

For a ring map \(\alpha:R\to R'\), the functor
\[
-\otimes_RR':
\mathsf{CommutativeAlgebras}(R)
\longrightarrow
\mathsf{CommutativeAlgebras}(R')
\]
is indexed by \(\alpha\).  It is not an internal morphism of either algebra
category.

Finitely generated and finitely presented are axioms on this category of
\(R\)-algebras.  They are not properties of the underlying ring alone.

### 4.7 Local rings

The object property and the morphism restriction are distinct.

- `CommutativeRings().Local()` is the replete full subcategory whose objects
  are rings with exactly one maximal ideal; its homsets contain all ring
  homomorphisms between such objects.
- `LocalRings()` has the same objects and only local homomorphisms.  The
  identity-on-objects functor
  \[
  \mathsf{LocalRings}\longrightarrow
  \mathsf{CommutativeRings.Local}
  \]
  is faithful and is not full.

A ring homomorphism \(f:(R,\mathfrak m)\to(S,\mathfrak n)\) is local exactly
when \(f(\mathfrak m)\subseteq\mathfrak n\), equivalently
\(f^{-1}(\mathfrak n)=\mathfrak m\).  See
[Stacks, Local rings](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/07BH).

Methods using only the unique maximal ideal belong to
`CommutativeRings().Local()`.  Methods functorial only for local
homomorphisms belong to `LocalRings().MorphismMethods`.

The corresponding joins with categories having local-ring axioms are:

```text
CommutativeRings().Local()
  .Noetherian()
  .Artinian()
  .Regular()
  .CohenMacaulay()
  .Gorenstein()
  .Complete()
  .Henselian()
  .DiscreteValuation()
```

Intersecting these object properties with `LocalRings()` gives the categories
with the same local-homomorphism restriction.

A discrete valuation ring is a Noetherian regular local domain of dimension
\(1\), not merely a ring together with a chosen valuation.

Every object of `CommutativeRings().Local()` supplies:

- its maximal ideal;
- its residue field and residue map.

Every local ring supplies the following mathematical constructions:

- Krull dimension;
- the cotangent space
  \(\mathfrak m/\mathfrak m^2\) and its dimension, the embedding dimension;
- the associated graded ring
  \(\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak m}R\);
- completion and completion map;
- punctured spectrum.

Depth is included on Noetherian local rings and finitely generated modules.
Algorithms for dimensions, depth, and explicit presentations of these
objects accept the coordinate presentation required by the computation; the
presentation is not part of the defining category.

`ElementMethods`:

- residue class;
- \(\mathfrak m\)-adic order for localizations
  \(A_{\mathfrak m}\) of finitely presented \(\mathbf Q\)-algebras, using
  exact membership in the powers of \(\mathfrak mA_{\mathfrak m}\), with
  order \(+\infty\) for zero;
- valuation on a discrete valuation ring;
- the decomposition \(x=u\pi^n\) only after a uniformizer \(\pi\) has been
  chosen.

`MorphismMethods` for a local map:

- induced residue-field homomorphism;
- induced linear map on cotangent spaces;
- induced map on associated graded rings;
- induced map on completions for Noetherian adic completions;
- composition as a local homomorphism.

Prime localization is the principal constructor:
\[
(R,\mathfrak p)
\longmapsto
\left(R_{\mathfrak p},
\mathfrak pR_{\mathfrak p},
\kappa(\mathfrak p),
R\to R_{\mathfrak p}\to\kappa(\mathfrak p)\right).
\]
The complete tuple of ordinary mathematical objects is returned.
The map \(R\to R_{\mathfrak p}\) is a morphism of commutative rings; it is
not generally a morphism of `LocalRings()` because \(R\) need not be local.
Even when \(R\) is local, localization at a nonmaximal prime is not a local
homomorphism.  The residue map
\(R_{\mathfrak p}\to\kappa(\mathfrak p)\) is local.

### 4.8 Modules, differentials, and graded algebras

The module categories supply kernels, cokernels, tensor products, symmetric
powers, exterior powers, finite presentations, and localization.  A ring map
\(\alpha:R\to S\) supplies the scalar-extension functor
\(-\otimes_RS:\mathsf{Modules}(R)\to\mathsf{Modules}(S)\).
The replete full subcategories needed here include:

- finitely generated;
- finitely presented;
- finite free of rank \(r\);
- finite locally free of rank \(r\);
- torsion-free and reflexive over rings for which these notions are fixed.

For a finitely presented module \(M\), its Fitting ideals are ordinary ideals
of the base ring and commute with base change.  They are the algebraic input
for the singular subschemes of morphisms used below.

The graded categories are:

- `GradedCommutativeAlgebras(R,G)`;
- `GradedModules(A,G)`;
- the replete full subcategory of connected nonnegatively graded algebras;
- the replete full subcategory of finitely generated graded algebras.

Here \(G\) may be \(\mathbf Z\), \(\mathbf Z^r\), or another specified abelian
grading group.  `Proj` belongs to the \(\mathbf Z_{\geq0}\)-graded theory.
Multigraded Proj and Cox constructions retain the grading group and irrelevant
ideal.

### 4.9 Preservation table for algebra

| Property | Localization | Quotient | Base extension / tensor product |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noetherian | preserved | preserved | if the new base is Noetherian, finite-type base extensions and tensor products of finite-type algebras are Noetherian |
| reduced | preserved | exactly when the quotient ideal is radical | not preserved in general |
| domain | preserved if \(0\notin S\) | exactly for prime quotient ideals | not preserved in general |
| normal | preserved | not preserved in general | not preserved without geometric normality |
| regular | preserved | not preserved by arbitrary quotients | a smooth algebra over a regular Noetherian ring is regular |
| Cohen-Macaulay | preserved | preserved by quotient by a regular sequence | for flat maps of Noetherian local rings, a Cohen-Macaulay base and Cohen-Macaulay closed fiber give a Cohen-Macaulay target |
| Gorenstein | preserved | preserved by quotient by a regular sequence | for flat maps of Noetherian local rings, a Gorenstein base and Gorenstein closed fiber give a Gorenstein target |

Over a perfect field, a reduced finite-type algebra is geometrically reduced.
This is why products of reduced finite-type \(\mathbf Q\)-schemes behave better
than products over an arbitrary field.  The category with the geometric
axiom, not the coordinate presentation, records that stability.

## 5. Schemes and scheme morphisms

### 5.1 Absolute schemes, slices, and arrows

`Schemes()` is the category of schemes and scheme morphisms.
For a scheme \(S\),
`Schemes(S)` is the slice category whose objects are structure morphisms
\(X\to S\).

`Arrows(Schemes())` has scheme morphisms as objects and commutative squares as
morphisms.  A property of morphisms defines a replete full subcategory of
this arrow category.  A property of an \(S\)-scheme is obtained by applying
the corresponding morphism property to its structure morphism.

For example:

\[
\mathsf{Schemes}(S).\mathsf{Smooth}
=
\{X\to S:\ X\to S\text{ is smooth}\}.
\]

This prevents `Smooth` from being an unexplained property of a bare object.

### 5.2 Replete full subcategories of schemes

The intrinsic replete full subcategories of schemes are:

```text
Schemes()
  .QuasiCompact()
  .QuasiSeparated()
  .LocallyNoetherian()
  .Noetherian()
  .Jacobson()
  .Nagata()
  .UniversallyCatenary()
  .QuasiExcellent()
  .Excellent()
  .Reduced()
  .Irreducible()
  .Connected()
  .Integral()
  .Normal()
  .Regular()
  .CohenMacaulay()
  .Gorenstein()
  .Equidimensional()
  .PureDimension(d)
```

`Regular`, `CohenMacaulay`, and `Gorenstein` here use the locally Noetherian
definitions and therefore are subcategories of `LocallyNoetherian`.
`Nagata`, `UniversallyCatenary`, `QuasiExcellent`, and `Excellent` mean that
the scheme admits an affine open cover by spectra of rings with the
corresponding property; equivalently, every affine open has that property.
`Jacobson` means that every closed subset has a dense set of closed points.

For schemes over a field \(k\), add:

```text
Schemes(k)
  .GeometricallyReduced()
  .GeometricallyIrreducible()
  .GeometricallyConnected()
  .GeometricallyIntegral()
  .GeometricallyNormal()
```

The principal implications are:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{Integral}
&\Longleftrightarrow
\mathsf{Reduced}\cap\mathsf{Irreducible};\\
\mathsf{Regular}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Normal}
\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Reduced};\\
\mathsf{Gorenstein}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{CohenMacaulay};\\
\mathsf{Excellent}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{QuasiExcellent}\cap\mathsf{UniversallyCatenary};\\
\mathsf{QuasiExcellent}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Nagata};\\
\mathsf{GeometricallyIntegral}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{GeometricallyIrreducible}
\cap
\mathsf{GeometricallyReduced};\\
\mathsf{GeometricallyNormal}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Normal}
\cap
\mathsf{GeometricallyReduced}.
\end{aligned}
\]

No implication from Cohen-Macaulay to reduced is introduced.

### 5.3 Replete full subcategories of scheme morphisms

The arrow category requires the following replete full subcategories:

```text
Arrows(Schemes())
  .QuasiCompact()
  .QuasiSeparated()
  .Affine()
  .OpenImmersion()
  .ClosedImmersion()
  .Immersion()
  .LocallyFiniteType()
  .FiniteType()
  .LocallyFinitePresentation()
  .FinitePresentation()
  .Separated()
  .UniversallyClosed()
  .QuasiFinite()
  .Finite()
  .FiniteLocallyFree(rank)
  .FiniteEtale()
  .Flat()
  .FaithfullyFlat()
  .Smooth()
  .Unramified()
  .Etale()
  .Dominant()
  .Birational()
  .Proper()
  .Projective()
  .LocalCompleteIntersection()
  .CohenMacaulay()
  .Gorenstein()
  .RelativePureDimension(d)
```

Standard implications include:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathsf{Etale}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Smooth}\cap\mathsf{Unramified};\\
\mathsf{Smooth}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Flat}\cap\mathsf{LocallyFinitePresentation};\\
\mathsf{Finite}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Affine}\cap\mathsf{Proper};\\
\mathsf{FiniteEtale}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{FiniteLocallyFree}\cap\mathsf{Etale};\\
\mathsf{Projective}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Proper};\\
\mathsf{ClosedImmersion}
&\Longrightarrow
\mathsf{Finite}\cap\mathsf{Separated};\\
\mathsf{Flat}\cap\mathsf{LocalCompleteIntersection}
&\Longrightarrow \mathsf{Gorenstein}
\Longrightarrow \mathsf{CohenMacaulay}
\end{aligned}
\]

For morphisms with locally Noetherian fibers, `CohenMacaulay` means flat with
Cohen-Macaulay fibers, and `Gorenstein` means flat with Gorenstein fibers.
Thus every Gorenstein morphism is Cohen-Macaulay.  A flat local
complete-intersection morphism, equivalently a syntomic morphism, is
Gorenstein.  Over a field, a local complete-intersection \(k\)-scheme has the
displayed Gorenstein and Cohen-Macaulay consequences.  See
[Stacks, Cohen-Macaulay morphisms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/045R),
[Gorenstein morphisms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0C04), and
[flat local complete-intersection morphisms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0C15).

Open immersions, closed immersions, affine morphisms, finite morphisms, flat
morphisms, smooth morphisms, etale morphisms, proper morphisms, and projective
morphisms are stable under arbitrary base change.  A local
complete-intersection morphism is stable under flat base change; its
intersection with flat morphisms is syntomic and is stable under arbitrary
base change.  These morphism classes are stable under composition.  See
[Stacks, base change of local complete-intersection
morphisms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/069I) and
[syntomic morphisms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01UB).
The category implications must preserve these different hypotheses.

### 5.4 `Schemes(S).ParentMethods`

Every \(S\)-scheme supplies:

- base scheme and structure morphism;
- `Hom_S(Y)` and `End_S()`;
- fiber product over \(S\);
- diagonal morphism;
- open and closed subschemes;
- the parent representing its topological space \(|X|\), while
  \(T\)-valued points are supplied by the homset
  \(\operatorname{Hom}_S(T,X)\);
- quasi-coherent module category;
- the collection of affine open covers with their gluing data; a particular
  cover is additional chosen data.

Restricted methods:

| Replete full subcategory of `Schemes(S)` | Additional methods |
| --- | --- |
| affine over \(S=\operatorname{Spec}R\) | coordinate \(R\)-algebra and anti-equivalence with commutative \(R\)-algebras |
| affine over general \(S\) | anti-equivalence with quasi-coherent \(\mathcal O_S\)-algebras through relative spectrum |
| locally Noetherian | irreducible components, associated points, local dimension |
| reduced | reduced affine coordinate rings and preservation under passage to open subschemes |
| integral | function field and generic point |
| normal | codimension-one valuation and Weil-divisor operations under Noetherian hypotheses |
| proper over a field | coherent cohomology with finite-dimensional outputs |
| projective | properness and stability consequences; embeddings and homogeneous presentations only when a closed immersion into a projective bundle is chosen |
| smooth | tangent bundle, relative differential bundle, smooth fibers |
| local complete intersection | cotangent-complex and conormal constructions |
| flat, locally finitely presented, with nonempty fibers equidimensional of dimension \(d\) | nonsmooth closed subscheme defined by \(\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{X/S})\) |

### 5.5 `Schemes(S).MorphismMethods`

For \(f:X\to Y\) over \(S\):

- domain, codomain, and structure-map compatibility;
- graph and, for \(f:X\to S\), fibers over point morphisms;
- base change along \(Y'\to Y\);
- inverse image of open, closed, locally closed, and constructible loci;
- pullback on quasi-coherent modules and invertible modules;
- induced maps on local rings at \(x\mapsto y\);
- induced residue-field maps;
- relative differentials;
- scheme-theoretic image, namely the smallest closed subscheme of \(Y\)
  through which \(f\) factors;
- direct image of constructible subsets under the hypotheses of Chevalley's
  theorem;
- proper image as a closed subset.

Pushforward of quasi-coherent modules is placed on quasi-compact
quasi-separated morphisms.  Pullback exists for every scheme morphism.
The scheme-theoretic image exists for every scheme morphism.  When \(f\) is
quasi-compact, its ideal sheaf is
\(\ker(\mathcal O_Y\to f_*\mathcal O_X)\), this kernel is quasi-coherent, the
construction restricts to open subschemes of \(Y\), and \(f(X)\) is dense in
the underlying closed subset of the image.

### 5.6 Point parents, not generic scheme `ElementMethods`

The architecture uses:

\[
\operatorname{Points}_S(T,X)
=
\operatorname{Hom}_S(T,X).
\]

Thus a \(T\)-valued point is a morphism and receives `MorphismMethods`.
`X.points(T)` returns the homset, not a second collection with weaker
semantics.

The topological points are instead elements of the parent representing
\(|X|\).  For \(x\in|X|\), that point parent supplies:

- closure and specialization relations;
- residue field;
- local ring;
- canonical point morphism
  \(\operatorname{Spec}\kappa(x)\to X\);
- membership in an open or closed subscheme.

There are no generic `Schemes(S).ElementMethods`.  An operation depending
only on \(x\in|X|\) belongs to the topological-point parent.  An operation
depending on the domain \(T\) belongs to the point morphism in
\(\operatorname{Hom}_S(T,X)\).

### 5.7 Reduced schemes in full detail

A scheme \(X\) is reduced exactly when every local ring
\(\mathcal O_{X,x}\) is reduced.  For an affine scheme this is equivalent to
its coordinate ring being reduced.  These are the definitions in
[Stacks, Reduced schemes](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01IZ).

Every scheme has a reduction
\[
i_X:X_{\mathrm{red}}\hookrightarrow X
\]
with the same topological space and nilradical ideal sheaf.  For every reduced
scheme \(T\), composition with \(i_X\) gives a bijection
\[
\operatorname{Hom}(T,X_{\mathrm{red}})
\xrightarrow{\sim}
\operatorname{Hom}(T,X).
\]
Thus reduction is right adjoint to the inclusion of reduced schemes.

`ReducedSchemes.ParentMethods` supplies consequences of reducedness, while
`Schemes.ParentMethods.reduction()` constructs \(X_{\mathrm{red}}\) and
\(i_X\).

`Schemes.MorphismMethods` supplies factorization through a target reduction
when the domain is reduced.

Preservation rules:

- open subschemes of reduced schemes are reduced;
- localization of a reduced ring is reduced;
- a closed subscheme is reduced exactly when its defining ideal sheaf is
  radical;
- arbitrary base change need not preserve reducedness;
- a geometrically reduced \(k\)-scheme remains reduced after every field
  extension;
- products of geometrically reduced \(k\)-schemes are geometrically reduced;
- for finite-type schemes over a perfect field, reducedness implies geometric
  reducedness.

These distinctions determine whether fiber products remain in the same
subcategory.  They also determine where the `GeometricallyReduced` category
is needed.  See
[Stacks, Geometrically reduced schemes](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/035U).

### 5.8 Normalization

For a reduced scheme with locally finitely many irreducible components, the
normalization is the relative spectrum of the integral closure in the product
of the component function fields.  It factors through the reduction of an
arbitrary scheme.  In the locally Noetherian cases used here it gives
\[
\nu:X^\nu\to X
\]
with \(X^\nu\) normal.  If \(X\) is Nagata, \(\nu\) is finite; in particular
this holds for a finite-type scheme over a field.

`normalization()` as an intrinsic construction belongs on the corresponding
reduced category and returns both \(X^\nu\) and \(\nu\).  The consequence that
\(\nu\) is finite belongs to the intersection with `Nagata`.  A method which
also presents \(X^\nu\) by equations is supplied for reduced finite-type
\(\mathbf Q\)-schemes with a chosen finite affine cover
\(\operatorname{Spec}A_i\), with each \(A_i\) finitely presented: normalize
the \(A_i\), use compatibility of normalization with restriction to open
subschemes to glue the chartwise results, and return the finite map.
Normalization is not asserted to commute with arbitrary base change.  See
[Stacks, Normalization](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/035E).

### 5.9 Smooth locus and singular closed subschemes

For a morphism \(f:X\to S\) locally of finite presentation, the smooth locus
\[
\operatorname{Sm}(X/S)\subseteq X
\]
is the largest open subscheme on which \(f\) is smooth.  Its complement is the
nonsmooth closed subset.  This is a statement about an open subscheme, not
yet a choice of nilpotent structure on the complement.  See
[Stacks, Smooth morphisms](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01V4).

For a flat, locally finitely presented morphism whose nonempty fibers are
equidimensional of dimension \(d\), the ideal
\[
\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{X/S})
\]
defines the relative singular subscheme
\(\operatorname{Sing}(X/S)\), whose underlying set is the nonsmooth locus.
For a hypersurface
\(f=0\) in a smooth \(S\)-scheme, this specializes on a coordinate chart to
the ideal generated by \(f\) and its relative partial derivatives.

The API therefore distinguishes:

- `smooth_locus()`, returning an open subscheme;
- `nonsmooth_closed_subset()`, returning the complementary closed subset;
- `relative_singular_subscheme()`, returning
  \(\operatorname{Sing}(X/S)\) cut out by
  \(\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{X/S})\), available on the stated flat,
  locally-finitely-presented, equidimensional-fiber category;
- a reduced induced closed subscheme on the complement, when that particular
  scheme structure is requested.

A generic `sing()` method on every scheme is not part of the language.

### 5.10 Affine spectrum and affine schemes

For a commutative ring \(R\),
\[
\operatorname{Spec}:
\mathsf{CAlg}_R^{\mathrm{op}}
\longrightarrow
\mathsf{AffineSchemes}(\operatorname{Spec}R)
\]
is an equivalence.  In particular,
\[
\operatorname{Hom}_{R\text{-alg}}(A,B)
\cong
\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{Spec}R}
(\operatorname{Spec}B,\operatorname{Spec}A).
\]

The constructions correspond as follows:

| Algebra | Geometry |
| --- | --- |
| quotient \(A\to A/I\) | closed immersion \(\operatorname{Spec}(A/I)\hookrightarrow\operatorname{Spec}A\) |
| localization \(A\to S^{-1}A\) | morphism into \(\operatorname{Spec}A\), an open immersion for the standard open-localization cases |
| tensor product \(A\otimes_RB\) | fiber product of affine \(R\)-schemes |
| prime \(\mathfrak p\) | point and local scheme \(\operatorname{Spec}A_{\mathfrak p}\) |
| radical ideal | reduced closed affine subscheme |

This equivalence with the opposite algebra category owns these translations.
Coordinate code does not
restate them separately.

### 5.11 Pointed schemes and local schemes

`PointedSchemes(S)` has objects \((X,x)\), with
\(X\to S\) a scheme and \(x\in|X|\).  A morphism
\[
f:(X,x)\to(Y,y)
\]
is an \(S\)-morphism satisfying \(f(x)=y\).  It induces:

\[
\mathcal O_{Y,y}\longrightarrow\mathcal O_{X,x},
\qquad
\kappa(y)\longrightarrow\kappa(x).
\]

The first is a local homomorphism.  Consequently,
\[
(X,x)\longmapsto\mathcal O_{X,x}
\]
is a contravariant functor from pointed schemes to local rings, while
\[
(X,x)\longmapsto\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal O_{X,x}
\]
is a covariant functor from pointed schemes to local schemes.
No ideal-equality computation is required to admit this map to the local-ring
homset: locality is part of the theorem on morphisms of locally ringed spaces.

The category of scheme germs has the same objects \((X,x)\), but a morphism

\[
(X,x)\longrightarrow(Y,y)
\]

is an equivalence class of scheme morphisms \(U\to Y\), where
\(U\subseteq X\) is an open neighborhood of \(x\) and \(x\) maps to \(y\);
two representatives are equal when they agree after shrinking around \(x\).
The functor from pointed schemes to scheme germs forgets behavior away from
the distinguished point.  The local-scheme functor factors through scheme
germs:

\[
(X,x)\longmapsto\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal O_{X,x}.
\]

Thus scheme germs and local schemes are related but are not identified by
definition.

For a field \(k\), the category of local \(k\)-schemes essentially of finite
type consists of local schemes
\(\operatorname{Spec}A\) with \(A\) a local \(k\)-algebra essentially of
finite type, and local \(k\)-morphisms.  It is anti-equivalent to the
corresponding category of local \(k\)-algebras.

The required replete full subcategories are those of reduced, integral,
normal, regular, Cohen-Macaulay, Gorenstein, local-complete-intersection,
hypersurface, and isolated-singularity local schemes.  Dimension and
embedding dimension instead index full subcategories:

- dimension \(d\) and embedding dimension \(e\).

The completed local ring defines the affine formal scheme
\(\operatorname{Spf}\widehat{\mathcal O}_{X,x}\), equivalently the formal
completion along the closed point in this local setting.  Affine formal
schemes form a separate category; completion is a functor from Noetherian
local schemes.  No method silently replaces a local scheme by its formal
completion.  See [Stacks, germs of schemes](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/04QR).

### 5.12 Affine open covers and gluing data

Gluing data for an affine open cover over \(S\) consist of:

- affine \(S\)-schemes \(U_i\);
- open subschemes \(U_{ij}\subseteq U_i\), with \(U_{ii}=U_i\);
- gluing isomorphisms
  \(\varphi_{ij}:U_{ij}\xrightarrow{\sim}U_{ji}\), with
  \(\varphi_{ii}=\operatorname{id}\) and
  \(\varphi_{ji}=\varphi_{ij}^{-1}\);
- for every \(i,j,k\),
  \[
  \varphi_{ij}(U_{ij}\cap U_{ik})=U_{ji}\cap U_{jk}
  \]
  and
  \[
  \varphi_{ik}
  =
  \varphi_{jk}\circ\varphi_{ij}
  \quad\text{on }U_{ij}\cap U_{ik}.
  \]

These objects and their compatible maps form the category of gluing data for
affine open covers over \(S\).  Its methods are:

- charts, overlaps, and truncated Cech nerve;
- a chosen cover with maps to two given covers;
- realization as a scheme;
- comparison morphism between realizations.

A base morphism \(S'\to S\) indexes the base-change functor on gluing data.

The gluing functor
\[
\operatorname{Glue}:
\mathsf{GluingData}_{\mathrm{aff}/S}\to\mathsf{Schemes}(S)
\]
forgets the chosen cover after constructing the scheme.  A scheme with two
different affine open covers remains one scheme with two choices of gluing
data.  This is the standard gluing construction of
[Stacks, Glueing schemes](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01JA).

### 5.13 Blowups and strict transforms

For a scheme \(X\) and a quasi-coherent ideal sheaf
\(\mathcal I\subseteq\mathcal O_X\), its Rees algebra is

\[
\mathcal R(\mathcal I)
=
\bigoplus_{n\geq0}\mathcal I^n.
\]

The blowup of \(X\) in the closed subscheme
\(Z=V(\mathcal I)\) is

\[
\operatorname{Bl}_Z(X)
=
\underline{\operatorname{Proj}}_X\mathcal R(\mathcal I),
\]

together with its morphism
\(\pi:\operatorname{Bl}_Z(X)\to X\).  The inverse image
\(\pi^{-1}(Z)\) is an effective Cartier divisor, and the blowup is universal
among morphisms \(Y\to X\) for which the inverse image of \(Z\) is an
effective Cartier divisor.  If \(\mathcal I\) is of finite type, \(\pi\) is
projective.

For a morphism \(f:W\to X\), the total transform is
\[
W\times_X\operatorname{Bl}_Z(X).
\]
Let \(E\) be the exceptional divisor.  The strict transform is the closed
subscheme of this fiber product cut out by the quasi-coherent ideal of local
sections supported on the inverse image of \(E\).  Equivalently, it is the
blowup of \(W\) in the inverse-image closed subscheme \(f^{-1}(Z)\).  For a
closed immersion \(W\hookrightarrow X\), the strict transform maps by a
closed immersion into \(\operatorname{Bl}_Z(X)\).  The total transform,
strict transform, and exceptional divisor are distinct outputs.

The Rees algebra, relative Proj, blowup morphism, exceptional divisor, and
strict transform belong to general scheme and quasi-coherent-algebra
categories.  Identification of a base change of the blowup with the blowup
of the pulled-back ideal is supplied under flat base change; it is not an
unconditional preservation rule.  See
[Stacks, blowing up](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01OF).
The definition of strict transform is
[Stacks, strict transform](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/080C).

## 6. Quasi-coherent modules, invertible modules, and divisors

### 6.1 Sheaf categories

For every scheme \(X\), `QCoh(X)` is the category of quasi-coherent
\(\mathcal O_X\)-modules and \(\mathcal O_X\)-linear maps.  If \(X\) is
locally Noetherian, `Coh(X)` is its full subcategory of coherent modules.

The required replete full subcategories are:

```text
QCoh(X)
  .FiniteType()
  .FinitePresentation()
  .Flat()
  .FiniteLocallyFree(rank)
  .Invertible()
  .TorsionFree()      # on an integral or otherwise specified base
  .Reflexive()        # on a Noetherian base
```

`FiniteLocallyFree(rank=1)` and `Invertible()` define the same replete full
subcategory.
The parameter \(r\) in `FiniteLocallyFree(rank=r)` remains visible.

The category `QCoh(X)` supplies its zero object, kernels, cokernels, images,
and finite limits and colimits.  For an object
\(\mathcal F\in\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\), `QCoh(X).ParentMethods` supplies:

- `base_scheme()`, `sections(U)`, `global_sections()`, `stalk(x)`, and
  `fiber(x)`;
- tensor product with another quasi-coherent module;
- sheaf Hom, with quasi-coherent output when the first argument is finitely
  presented;
- symmetric and exterior powers;
- restriction to an open subscheme;
- support, annihilator ideal sheaf, and Fitting ideal sheaves for finitely
  presented modules;
- sheaf cohomology; when \(X\to\operatorname{Spec}R\), each
  \(H^i(X,\mathcal F)\) is naturally an \(R\)-module.

For a finitely presented quasi-coherent module, the sheaf dual
\(\mathcal F^\vee=\mathcal Hom(\mathcal F,\mathcal O_X)\) is
quasi-coherent.  Duality as an involution, compatibility with arbitrary
pullback, and determinant belong to the finite locally free categories; the
determinant of a rank-\(r\) object lands in `InvertibleModules(X)`.

There are no generic `QCoh(X).ElementMethods` for “a local section” without
an open set.  If \(s\in\Gamma(U,\mathcal F)\), it is an element of
`Sections(U,F)`.  Its methods are restriction, germ, and image under a sheaf
map.  If \(U=X\), it is a global section; zero-scheme methods are added only
when \(\mathcal F\) is invertible.

For \(f:X\to Y\), pullback is a functor
\[
f^*:\mathsf{QCoh}(Y)\to\mathsf{QCoh}(X).
\]
If \(f\) is quasi-compact and quasi-separated, pushforward preserves
quasi-coherence:
\[
f_*:\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\to\mathsf{QCoh}(Y).
\]
The two operations have different variance and hypotheses.

### 6.2 Invertible modules and the Picard group

`InvertibleModules(X)` is the full subcategory of `QCoh(X)` on invertible
modules, with all sheaf homomorphisms.  Its isomorphisms form
the Picard groupoid \(\mathbf{Pic}(X)\).  The Picard group is
\[
\operatorname{Pic}(X)
=
\pi_0\!\left(\mathbf{Pic}(X)\right)
\]
with tensor product.

This distinction matters:

- an arbitrary map of invertible modules need not be an isomorphism;
- the Picard group forgets automorphisms and representatives;
- an effective Cartier divisor or Cartier divisor determines an invertible
  module, whereas a Weil divisor on a locally Noetherian normal integral
  scheme determines a rank-one reflexive module and determines an invertible
  module exactly when it is Cartier;
- an embedded presentation may provide generators for \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)\)
  without determining the intrinsic Picard group.

`InvertibleModules(X).ParentMethods`:

- tensor product with another invertible module and dual;
- isomorphisms in the Picard groupoid;
- passage to the Picard class;
- global sections.

The category of invertible modules supplies the tensor unit
\(\mathcal O_X\).  `PicardGroup(X).ParentMethods` supplies the zero class.
For \(f:X\to Y\), the morphism \(f\) supplies the pullback functors on
invertible modules and the homomorphism
\(f^*:\operatorname{Pic}(Y)\to\operatorname{Pic}(X)\).
A presentation by generators and relations is
additional mathematical data unless the category of \(X\) supplies a
particular computation of \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)\).  Its elements inherit
addition and inverse from the abelian group structure and also supply:

- degree on proper integral curves;
- intersection with another Picard class on smooth proper surfaces over a
  field.

### 6.3 Sections and zero schemes

For \(\mathcal F\in\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\),
\[
\Gamma(X,\mathcal F)=H^0(X,\mathcal F)
\]
is a module.  A section is an element of that module.

For \(s\in H^0(X,L)\), where \(L\) is invertible, the zero scheme \(Z(s)\) is
the closed subscheme defined locally by the image of
\(L^{-1}\xrightarrow{s^\vee}\mathcal O_X\).  This exists for every section.

A section is regular if multiplication by its local equation is injective in
every stalk.  Equivalently, its local equations are non-zero-divisors.  The
regular sections form `RegularSections(X,L)`, a distinguished subset of
\(H^0(X,L)\).

A regular section determines an effective Cartier divisor.  Conversely, an
effective Cartier divisor \(D\) determines
\(\mathcal O_X(D)\) with its canonical regular section.  Up to isomorphism of
pairs, these constructions are inverse.  See:

- [Stacks, Regular sections](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01WY);
- [Stacks, Effective Cartier divisors](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0CPG);
- [Stacks, Divisors and invertible sheaves](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/0847).

For a scheme with a chosen finite affine cover
\(U_i=\operatorname{Spec}A_i\), where each \(A_i\) is a finitely presented
\(\mathbf Q\)-algebra, and chosen trivializations of \(L|_{U_i}\), regularity
of \(s\) is decidable: compute the associated primes of every \(A_i\) and
check that the local equation of \(s\) lies in none of them.  This is an
operation on the stated presentation of \((X,L,s)\); the resulting regular
section lies in the intrinsic distinguished subset
`RegularSections(X,L)`.

The method placement is:

| Object | Method | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| any section \(s\) of \(L\) | `zero_scheme()` | closed subscheme of \(X\) |
| regular section \(s\) | `effective_cartier_divisor()` | effective Cartier divisor |
| effective Cartier divisor \(D\) | `invertible_module()` | \(\mathcal O_X(D)\) |
| effective Cartier divisor \(D\) | `canonical_section()` | regular section of \(\mathcal O_X(D)\) |

### 6.4 Divisor parents

For fixed \(X\):

- `EffectiveCartierDivisors(X)` is a commutative monoid;
- `CartierDivisors(X)` is an abelian group;
- when \(X\) is locally Noetherian and integral, `WeilDivisors(X)` consists
  of locally finite integral sums of codimension-one integral closed
  subschemes;
- when \(X\) is Noetherian and integral, these sums are finite, so
  `WeilDivisors(X)` is the free abelian group on the prime divisors;
- for locally Noetherian integral \(X\),
  `WeilDivisorClassGroup(X)` is the quotient of `WeilDivisors(X)` by principal
  Weil divisors.

On a locally Noetherian integral scheme there is a canonical homomorphism
\(\operatorname{Pic}(X)\to\operatorname{Cl}(X)\).  If every local ring of
\(X\) is a unique factorization domain, it is an isomorphism.  If \(X\) is
also normal, \(\operatorname{Cl}(X)\) is the group of isomorphism classes of
rank-one coherent reflexive modules.  These comparison maps do not identify
the groups in greater generality.

Method ownership for the fixed-\(X\) parents is:

| Parent | `ParentMethods` | `ElementMethods` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `EffectiveCartierDivisors(X)` | zero divisor and the homomorphism to \(\operatorname{Pic}(X)\) | ideal sheaf, support, sum, \(\mathcal O_X(D)\), and canonical regular section |
| `CartierDivisors(X)` | zero divisor, principal-divisor homomorphism, and class maps | local meromorphic equations, sum, inverse, support, and \(\mathcal O_X(D)\) |
| `WeilDivisors(X)` | zero divisor, prime divisors, principal-divisor homomorphism, and quotient map to `WeilDivisorClassGroup(X)` | coefficient at a prime divisor, support, sum, and inverse |

Pullback and pushforward are homomorphisms between divisor parents on
different schemes.  They belong to the scheme-morphism categories listed
immediately below, not to a divisor element with an unspecified map.

Pullback rules:

- invertible modules pull back along every morphism;
- the inverse image of a closed subscheme, hence of a zero scheme, exists
  along every morphism;
- for \(f:X\to Y\) and an effective Cartier divisor \(D\subseteq Y\),
  \(f^*D\) is defined as an effective Cartier divisor exactly when the
  inverse-image closed subscheme \(f^{-1}(D)\) is an effective Cartier
  divisor on \(X\);
- this condition holds when \(f\) is flat;
- it also holds when \(X,Y\) are integral and \(f\) is dominant; when \(X\)
  is reduced and no generic point of an irreducible component maps into
  \(D\); or when \(X\) is locally Noetherian and no associated point maps
  into \(D\);
- for a nonflat map outside these conditions, pullback of a regular section
  need not remain regular;
- pullback of a general Cartier divisor is supplied only on morphism
  categories for which the local meromorphic equations pull back to
  meromorphic equations.

The API therefore lets any morphism pull back \(L\), \(s\), and the
inverse-image closed subscheme.  It returns an effective Cartier divisor
only on the morphism categories above, or after the finite-affine
associated-prime computation of Section 6.3 establishes regularity of the
pulled-back section.  See
[Stacks, pullback of effective Cartier divisors](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01WQ).

### 6.5 Principal parts, jets, and quadratic terms

Let \(g:Y\to B\) be a morphism and let
\(\Delta:Y\to Y\times_B Y\) have ideal sheaf \(\mathcal I_\Delta\).
For an \(\mathcal O_Y\)-module \(L\), the sheaf of principal parts of order
\(m\) is

\[
\mathcal P^m_{Y/B}(L)
=
(p_1)_*\left(
  \bigl(\mathcal O_{Y\times_B Y}/\mathcal I_\Delta^{m+1}\bigr)
  \otimes p_2^*L
\right).
\]

It represents relative differential operators of order at most \(m\).
The universal operator sends a section \(s\) to its jet

\[
j^m(s)\in H^0\!\left(Y,\mathcal P^m_{Y/B}(L)\right).
\]

The quotient maps of principal-parts sheaves remember truncation:

\[
\mathcal P^m_{Y/B}(L)\longrightarrow
\mathcal P^{m-1}_{Y/B}(L).
\]

For \(m=1\) there is the principal-parts sequence

\[
0\longrightarrow
\Omega_{Y/B}\otimes L
\longrightarrow
\mathcal P^1_{Y/B}(L)
\longrightarrow
L
\longrightarrow0.
\]

If \(g\) is smooth, the diagonal is a regular immersion and

\[
\mathcal I_\Delta^m/\mathcal I_\Delta^{m+1}
\cong
\operatorname{Sym}^m\Omega_{Y/B}.
\]

Consequently the kernel of
\(\mathcal P^2_{Y/B}(L)\to\mathcal P^1_{Y/B}(L)\) is
\(\operatorname{Sym}^2\Omega_{Y/B}\otimes L\).

Now let \(\sigma:B\to Y\) be a section.  On a parameter subscheme on which
\(\sigma^*j^1(s)=0\), the restricted second jet
\(\sigma^*j^2(s)\) lies in the pullback of
\(\ker(\mathcal P^2_{Y/B}(L)\to\mathcal P^1_{Y/B}(L))\).  Under the
isomorphism above it determines the degree-two term

\[
q_\sigma(s)\in
H^0\!\left(
  B,
  \operatorname{Sym}^2(\sigma^*\Omega_{Y/B})
  \otimes\sigma^*L
\right).
\]

Suppose \(g\) has relative dimension \(2\) and \(2\) is invertible on \(B\).
Put \(E=\sigma^*\Omega_{Y/B}\) and \(M=\sigma^*L\).  Polarization of this
quadratic form defines its Hessian bilinear map

\[
b_{q_\sigma(s)}:
E^\vee\longrightarrow E\otimes M.
\]

Its determinant is a section of

\[
(\det E)^{\otimes2}\otimes M^{\otimes2}.
\]

Define

\[
\operatorname{disc}(q_\sigma(s))
=
-\det(b_{q_\sigma(s)}).
\]

This is a section of the same invertible module, and its nonvanishing locus
is an intrinsic open subscheme.  In local parameters \(u,v\), if

\[
q=a u^2+buv+c v^2,
\]

then \(\operatorname{disc}(q)=b^2-4ac\).  For a relative effective Cartier
curve in a smooth relative surface, vanishing of the first jet together with
this nondegenerate degree-two term is the ordinary-double-point condition
along \(\sigma\).

Principal-parts sheaves and jet truncations belong to quasi-coherent-module
categories.  For fixed \(E\) and \(M\), the quadratic forms are the module
\[
H^0(B,\operatorname{Sym}^2E\otimes M),
\]
and the discriminant is an `ElementMethod` on this section parent when
\(\operatorname{rank}E=2\) and \(2\) is invertible on \(B\).  For a family of
effective Cartier curves in a smooth relative surface, the open
\(D(\operatorname{disc}(q_\sigma(s)))\) is the locus where the curve has an
ordinary double point along \(\sigma\).  No chart-specific Hessian is
promoted to a general scheme method.  See
[Stacks, finite-order differential operators and principal parts](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/09CQ).

### 6.6 Section rings, multisection rings, and Cox sheaves

For a pair \((X,L)\), the section ring is
\[
R(X,L)=\bigoplus_{n\geq0}H^0(X,L^{\otimes n}).
\]
For a morphism
\[
(f,\phi):(X,L_X)\longrightarrow(Y,L_Y),
\qquad
\phi:L_X\xrightarrow{\sim}f^*L_Y,
\]
pullback followed by \(\phi^{-1}:f^*L_Y\to L_X\) gives
\[
R(Y,L_Y)\longrightarrow R(X,L_X).
\]
Thus the section ring is a contravariant graded-ring functor on polarized
schemes.  It is not an operation on a bare scheme with an unspecified
invertible module.

For a subgroup \(\Lambda\subseteq\operatorname{Pic}(X)\) and chosen
representatives \(L_\lambda\), the multisection ring is
\[
\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda}H^0(X,L_\lambda).
\]
Multiplication requires coherent choices
\(L_\lambda\otimes L_\mu\cong L_{\lambda+\mu}\).
This is a multisection ring attached to the chosen representatives and
multiplication maps.  It is not, merely by that formula, a Cox ring.

For the standard Cox-sheaf construction, let \(X\) be a normal prevariety
over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero, assume
\(\Gamma(X,\mathcal O_X^\times)=k^\times\), and assume that
\(\operatorname{Cl}(X)\) is finitely generated.  Choose a subgroup
\[
K\subseteq\operatorname{WDiv}(X)
\]
whose class map \(c:K\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Cl}(X)\) is
surjective.  Put \(K^0=\ker(c)\), and choose a character
\[
\chi:K^0\longrightarrow k(X)^\times,
\qquad
\operatorname{div}(\chi(E))=E.
\]
The associated sheaf of divisorial algebras is
\[
\mathcal S=\bigoplus_{D\in K}\mathcal O_X(D).
\]
Let \(\mathcal I_\chi\) be the sheaf of ideals locally generated by
\(1-\chi(E)\) for \(E\in K^0\), with \(\chi(E)\) regarded as homogeneous of
degree \(-E\).  Then
\[
\mathcal R=\mathcal S/\mathcal I_\chi
\]
is the Cox sheaf with its \(\operatorname{Cl}(X)\)-grading, and the Cox ring
is \(\Gamma(X,\mathcal R)\).

Thus `divisorial_algebra(K)`, `cox_sheaf(K, character=chi)`, and
`cox_ring(K, character=chi)` belong only to the normal objects satisfying
these hypotheses, together with the chosen subgroup \(K\) and character
\(\chi\).  They are not methods on every scheme.  The construction and its
hypotheses follow
[Arzhantsev--Derenthal--Hausen--Laface, *Cox Rings*, Constructions 4.1.1 and
4.2.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.4229).

## 7. Projective geometry, curves, and surfaces

### 7.1 Projective and embedded objects

`ProjectiveMorphisms()` is the replete full subcategory of the scheme-arrow
category on projective morphisms.  `ProjectiveSchemes(S)` is its inverse
image under the structure-morphism functor.

The following categories of chosen data are distinct:

| Category | Object |
| --- | --- |
| `PolarizedSchemes(S)` | \((X\to S,L)\) with \(X\to S\) proper and finitely presented and \(L\) relatively ample |
| `EmbeddedProjectiveSchemes(S)` | a closed immersion \(X\hookrightarrow\mathbf P_S(E)\) |
| `EmbeddedSchemes(P)` | a closed immersion \(X\hookrightarrow P\) into fixed \(P\) |
| closed subschemes of \(\prod_i\mathbf P^{n_i}_k\) with chosen embedding | the closed immersion and multihomogeneous ideal |

Degree, multidegree, homogeneous ideal, irrelevant ideal, and a graded free
resolution of the homogeneous coordinate ring or ideal belong to an embedded
object.  They are not intrinsic methods on the underlying projective scheme.

`PolarizedSchemes.ParentMethods`:

- section ring;
- tensor powers of the polarization;
- over a field \(k\), the Hilbert polynomial
  \(n\mapsto\chi(X,L^{\otimes n})\);
- for a flat polarized family, the locally constant fiberwise Hilbert
  polynomial.

A finite locally free submodule of sections that generates \(L\) is additional
data.  It supplies the corresponding morphism to a projective bundle; the
polarization alone does not choose that submodule.

`EmbeddedProjectiveSchemes.ParentMethods`:

- homogeneous coordinate ideal and ring;
- conormal module;
- graded free resolution and Hilbert polynomial for a finitely generated
  homogeneous presentation over \(\mathbf Q\);
- underlying scheme and embedding morphism.

A base morphism \(S'\to S\) indexes base change of the embedded scheme and
its embedding arrow.

Products of projective spaces form a useful family of targets inside this
theory.  They are not the definition of projective geometry.

For

\[
P=\prod_{i=1}^r\mathbf P_k^{n_i},
\]

the chosen product presentation has Cox coordinate ring

\[
S=k[x_{i,j}:1\leq i\leq r,\ 0\leq j\leq n_i],
\qquad
\deg(x_{i,j})=e_i\in\mathbf Z^r,
\]

and irrelevant ideal

\[
B=\bigcap_{i=1}^r(x_{i,0},\ldots,x_{i,n_i}).
\]

A multihomogeneous ideal \(I\subseteq S\) gives a closed subscheme of \(P\)
through sheafification on the standard affine charts.  The saturation

\[
I^{\mathrm{sat}}=(I:B^\infty)
\]

gives the same closed subscheme and is the appropriate ideal for recovering
global multihomogeneous equations.  The multigrading, irrelevant ideal,
saturation, and chart dehomogenizations belong to the chosen embedding into
\(P\); the underlying scheme does not remember them.

### 7.2 Curves as a category join

For a field \(k\),
\[
\mathsf{Curves}(k)
=
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{FiniteType}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{Separated}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{PureDimension}(1).
\]

Named joins include:

- reduced, integral, geometrically reduced, and geometrically integral
  curves;
- proper and projective curves;
- smooth proper geometrically connected curves;
- Cohen-Macaulay and Gorenstein curves;
- local complete-intersection curves;
- nodal curves.

Methods are placed as follows.

| Curve category | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| locally Noetherian reduced | irreducible components and normalization |
| reduced Nagata | finite normalization, conductor ideal, branches, and local \(\delta\)-invariants where the relevant quotient has finite length |
| integral | function field and generic point |
| proper | arithmetic genus \(p_a(C)=1-\chi(C,\mathcal O_C)\) |
| smooth proper geometrically connected | genus, canonical invertible module, Riemann-Roch data |
| proper Cohen-Macaulay | dualizing module |
| Gorenstein | dualizing invertible module and Cartier canonical class |
| nodal | nodes, normalization preimages, dual graph, delta invariants |
| effective Cartier curve in a smooth surface | invertible normal module, intersection class, and adjunction |

If \(C\) is proper and geometrically connected with
\(H^0(C,\mathcal O_C)=k\), then
\(p_a(C)=\dim_kH^1(C,\mathcal O_C)\).

The term "genus" is not used without fixing whether it means arithmetic genus,
geometric genus of a normalization, or genus of a smooth proper curve.

For a reduced Nagata curve, finite normalization
\(\nu:C^\nu\to C\) gives the finite module

\[
\nu_*\mathcal O_{C^\nu}/\mathcal O_C.
\]

Its annihilator is the conductor ideal.  At a closed point \(p\) where this
quotient has finite length,

\[
\delta_p(C)
=
\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O_{C,p}}
\left(
  (\nu_*\mathcal O_{C^\nu}/\mathcal O_C)_p
\right).
\]

The points of \(C^\nu\) above \(p\) are the branches at \(p\).  For a
geometrically connected proper reduced curve over an algebraically closed
field, with \(r\) irreducible components and smooth normalizations of genera
\(g_1,\ldots,g_r\),

\[
p_a(C)=\sum_i g_i+\sum_p\delta_p(C)-r+1.
\]

This formula is a notebook computation to recover, not a replacement for the
definitions of arithmetic genus, normalization, or \(\delta_p\).

### 7.3 Surfaces as a category join

For a field \(k\),
\[
\mathsf{Surfaces}(k)
=
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{FiniteType}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{Separated}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(k).\mathsf{PureDimension}(2).
\]

Named joins include reduced, integral, normal, regular, smooth, proper,
projective, Cohen-Macaulay, Gorenstein, and local complete-intersection
surfaces.

Methods are placed as follows.

| Surface category | Methods |
| --- | --- |
| proper smooth | intersection pairing on Cartier divisor classes, Chern numbers, surface Riemann-Roch |
| normal proper | Weil divisor class group and codimension-one valuations |
| proper Cohen-Macaulay | dualizing module |
| Gorenstein proper | canonical invertible module and Cartier canonical class |
| smooth with effective Cartier curve \(C\) | adjunction and normal bundle of \(C\) |
| smooth with two effective Cartier curves meeting properly at \(p\) | local intersection multiplicity |
| projectively embedded | Hilbert polynomial and coordinate computations from the embedding |

`is_del_Pezzo()` belongs only after choosing the standard definition for the
relevant smooth or singular category.  Smooth del Pezzo surfaces, Gorenstein
del Pezzo surfaces, and log del Pezzo surfaces are different replete full
subcategories.

If \(C,D\) are effective Cartier curves on a smooth surface \(X\), with
isolated intersection at \(p\), and \(f,g\in\mathcal O_{X,p}\) are local
equations, then

\[
i_p(C,D;X)
=
\operatorname{length}_{\mathcal O_{X,p}}
\mathcal O_{X,p}/(f,g).
\]

The finiteness of this length is the proper-intersection condition at \(p\).
The value is independent of the chosen local equations.  This method
therefore belongs to the category of a smooth surface together with two
effective Cartier divisors meeting properly, not to arbitrary pairs of
closed subschemes.

### 7.4 The standard geometry of \(\mathbf P^1\times\mathbf P^1\)

The product
\[
Y=\mathbf P^1_k\times\mathbf P^1_k
\]
is a concrete object of the smooth projective surface category, with
projections \(\operatorname{pr}_1,\operatorname{pr}_2\).

The notation
\[
\mathcal O_Y(a,b)
=
\operatorname{pr}_1^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P^1}(a)
\otimes
\operatorname{pr}_2^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P^1}(b)
\]
belongs to this object's standard geometry.  It supplies:

\[
\operatorname{Pic}(Y)\cong\mathbf Z^2,\qquad
(a,b)\cdot(c,d)=ad+bc,\qquad
K_Y=\mathcal O_Y(-2,-2).
\]

For \(a,b\geq0\),
\[
h^0(Y,\mathcal O_Y(a,b))=(a+1)(b+1).
\]
For a smooth effective Cartier divisor \(C\) of bidegree \((a,b)\), adjunction
gives
\[
2p_a(C)-2=C\cdot(C+K_Y),
\qquad
p_a(C)=(a-1)(b-1).
\]
If \(C\) is also geometrically connected, then its arithmetic genus is its
genus.

These methods are obtained from products, pullbacks, Picard groups,
intersection theory, and adjunction.  A bespoke product-projective line-bundle
class is unnecessary.

## 8. Germs and singularities

### 8.1 Intrinsic germs and chosen hypersurface equations

The category of scheme germs over \(k\) contains arbitrary germs
\((X,x)\) of finite-type \(k\)-schemes.  The local-scheme functor sends such
a germ to \(\operatorname{Spec}\mathcal O_{X,x}\).  Replete full
subcategories of local \(k\)-schemes essentially of finite type are
determined by intrinsic properties of the local ring.

A coordinate calculation needs more data.  A hypersurface germ embedded in a
smooth germ, together with a chosen local equation, consists of:

\[
(Q,\mathfrak n,f),\qquad
Q=\mathcal O_{W,p}\text{ for a smooth finite-type }k\text{-scheme }W,\quad
f\in\mathfrak n,
\]
and represents \(\operatorname{Spec}(Q/(f))\) at its closed point.
Morphisms retain the local maps and compatibility with the hypersurface
ideals.  Isomorphisms allow changes of regular parameters and multiplication
of \(f\) by a unit.

The forgetful functor sends this chosen embedded hypersurface presentation to
its scheme germ and its local scheme.  The chosen equation supplies the
relative Jacobian ideal

\[
J_{Q/k}(f)
=
\operatorname{im}\!\left(
\operatorname{Der}_k(Q,Q)\xrightarrow{D\mapsto D(f)}Q
\right).
\]

In chosen regular parameters this ideal is generated by the partial
derivatives of \(f\).  The presentation supplies:

- Milnor algebra \(Q/J_{Q/k}(f)\) and Tjurina algebra
  \(Q/(f,J_{Q/k}(f))\), with numerical dimensions on the isolated
  singularity subcategory when these quotients have finite length;
- order and initial form;
- tangent cone;
- Hessian of the quadratic initial form;
- coordinate changes and completed normal-form calculations.

These are not generic methods on an arbitrary scheme point.

### 8.2 Dimension-specific joins

A plane curve hypersurface germ with a chosen equation has
\(\dim Q=2\).  A hypersurface surface germ with a chosen equation has
\(\dim Q=3\).  In both cases the intrinsic germ and the embedding in the
smooth germ remain distinct data.

Arbitrary singularities in varieties remain in the category of scheme germs
and its local-scheme image; the foundational local language is not restricted
to plane curves.

### 8.3 Isolated singularities and \(A_n\)

For a hypersurface germ with a chosen local equation, an isolated singularity
means that the closed point is singular and that the support of the singular
closed subscheme is exactly that closed point.  If
\(X=\operatorname{Spec}(Q/(f))\) is a hypersurface in a smooth
\(k\)-scheme, this closed subscheme has local algebra

\[
Q/(f,J_{Q/k}(f)),
\]

the Tjurina algebra.  The Milnor algebra \(Q/J_{Q/k}(f)\) is a different
quotient.  Requiring only containment would incorrectly include a smooth
germ, whose singular closed subscheme is empty.  The isolated hypersurface
singularities form a replete full subcategory of the category retaining the
embedding and equation.

Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic \(0\), a plane
hypersurface germ has type \(A_n\) when its completed local equation is
formally equivalent to
\[
u^2+v^{n+1}.
\]
Over a nonclosed field, the corresponding replete full subcategory consists
of plane curve germs geometrically of type \(A_n\): after scalar extension
to an algebraic closure of the residue field, the completed equation has the
displayed form.  The integer \(n\) remains part of the condition.

For \(n=1\) and characteristic different from \(2\), a plane hypersurface
germ has geometric type \(A_1\) when its quadratic initial form is
nondegenerate.  This is the local criterion used in the invariant-curve
program.

There is no generic ADE classifier on every local scheme.  Membership in the
subcategory geometrically of type \(A_n\) is established only in either of
the following circumstances:

1. a construction already known to produce that normal form;
2. an explicit automorphism of the completed power-series ring carries the
   chosen equation to \(u^2+v^{n+1}\) after the stated scalar extension.

For \(A_1\) in characteristic different from \(2\), the nondegeneracy
calculation for the quadratic initial form is the separate decision
procedure used here; no completed coordinate change needs to be guessed.

Milnor or Tjurina number alone is not treated as a complete classifier.

### 8.4 Germ method placement

| Category or parent | `ParentMethods` or object methods | `MorphismMethods` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| scheme germs \((X,x)\) | distinguished point, local scheme, residue field, dimension, and embedding dimension | equivalence classes of neighborhood morphisms, composition on equivalence classes, and induced local-scheme map |
| local \(k\)-schemes essentially of finite type | local ring, residue field, reduction, and completion | local-ring map, residue-field map, cotangent map, and completed map |
| reduced local \(k\)-schemes essentially of finite type | finite normalization as an affine scheme finite over the local scheme, and the inverse image of its closed point | ordinary local-scheme maps; no induced map on normalizations for an arbitrary morphism |
| integral local \(k\)-schemes essentially of finite type with dominant morphisms | the same finite normalization | induced morphisms of normalizations |
| finite-type pure-dimensional local schemes over \(k\) of dimension \(d\) | singular subscheme of \(X\to\operatorname{Spec}k\), cut out by \(\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{X/k})\); when \(k\) is perfect, its underlying set is the nonregular locus | maps induced by field extension on the singular subscheme |
| local complete-intersection local schemes | cotangent complex, conormal sequence, and tangent space | induced cotangent maps |
| hypersurface local schemes embedded in smooth local schemes with chosen equations | defining equation, Jacobian ideal, initial form, tangent cone, Milnor algebra, and Tjurina algebra | contact equivalences and induced completed maps |

The category of affine formal schemes owns formal equivalence.  The category
of local schemes owns algebraic maps.  Passing between a Noetherian local
scheme and its formal spectrum is explicit.

## 9. Group actions and equivariant geometry

### 9.1 Abstract groups and group schemes

For an abstract group \(G\) and category \(C\),
\[
\mathsf{Actions}(G,C)=[BG,C].
\]
An object is a functor, and a morphism is a natural transformation, hence an
equivariant morphism.

For a group scheme \(G\to S\), an action on \(X\to S\) is a morphism
\[
a:G\times_SX\to X
\]
satisfying the unit and associativity diagrams.  This category is distinct
from the functor category for an abstract group, even when the associated
constant group scheme relates the two.

For an abstract group \(G\) and field \(k\),

\[
\mathsf{Rep}_k(G)=[BG,\mathsf{Vect}_k].
\]

Its objects are \(k\)-linear representations and its morphisms are
intertwiners.  It supplies direct sums, tensor products, duals, invariant
subspaces

\[
V^G=\{v\in V:g v=v\text{ for every }g\in G\},
\]

and the corresponding functor on intertwiners.  If \(G\) is finite and
\(|G|\) is invertible in \(k\), the Reynolds operator is

\[
\rho_G(v)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}gv,
\]

and Maschke's theorem makes the representation category semisimple.  For a
finite abelian group over a splitting field, character eigenspaces give the
isotypic decomposition.  Without these hypotheses, invariant subspaces still
exist but neither this averaging formula nor a direct-sum decomposition into
characters is asserted.

### 9.2 Action methods

For a \(G\)-scheme \(X\):

`ParentMethods`:

- acting group and underlying scheme;
- action morphism;
- automorphism associated to an element of an abstract group;
- for a group-scheme action and a \(T\)-valued point, the orbit morphism
  \(G_T\to X_T\) and its stabilizer fiber product;
- fixed-point functor;
- fixed subscheme only on the replete full subcategory of actions for which
  that functor is represented by a scheme;
- restriction to an invariant open or closed subscheme;
- quotient on a category where the quotient construction is defined.

`MorphismMethods`:

- underlying scheme morphism;
- equivariance square;
- induced natural transformation on fixed-point functors, and the induced map
  of fixed subschemes when both are represented;
- induced map on quotient objects;
- pullback of equivariant sheaves.

For a single automorphism \(\tau:X\to X\), the fixed subscheme is the
equalizer
\[
X^\tau\longrightarrow X
\rightrightarrows X
\]
of \(\tau\) and \(\operatorname{id}_X\).  If \(X\) is separated, it is a closed
subscheme.  A list of fixed rational points is a computation of points of this
scheme, not its definition.

For a finite abstract group \(G\), the fixed subscheme is
\[
X^G=\bigcap_{g\in G}^{\mathrm{scheme}}
\operatorname{Eq}(g,\operatorname{id}_X).
\]
It is closed when \(X\) is separated.  For a group scheme \(G\to S\), the
fixed-point functor assigns to an \(S\)-scheme \(T\) the morphisms
\(x:T\to X\) for which the two maps
\[
G_T
\xrightarrow{\operatorname{id}_G\times x}
G\times_SX
\xrightarrow{a}
X
\quad\text{and}\quad
G_T\xrightarrow{\operatorname{pr}_T}T\xrightarrow{x}X
\]
are equal.  The equalizer of \(a\) and
\(\operatorname{pr}_2:G\times_SX\to X\) is instead the stabilizer group
scheme of the action inside \(G\times_SX\).  A group-scheme action supplies
`fixed_subscheme()` only after a representability theorem places it in the
corresponding replete full subcategory.

### 9.3 Equivariant sheaves and linearizations

`EquivariantQCoh(G,X)` contains quasi-coherent modules with descent
isomorphisms satisfying the cocycle condition.

For an automorphism \(\tau\) and invertible module \(L\), a linearization is
an isomorphism
\[
\lambda:\tau^*L\xrightarrow{\sim}L
\]
satisfying the group-law cocycle condition.  These objects and their
equivariant isomorphisms form the Picard groupoid of \(G\)-linearized
invertible modules.

A linearization induces:

- a representation on \(H^0(X,L)\);
- the invariant submodule;
- isotypic summands only when the representation category is semisimple, for
  example for a finite group over a field whose characteristic does not
  divide the group order;
- equivariant pullback and tensor product;
- evaluation maps compatible with stabilizer representations on fibers.

An action on \(X\) alone does not determine an action on \(H^0(X,L)\).

### 9.4 Quotients

Quotient methods are restricted by mathematical construction.

- For a finite group acting on an affine scheme \(\operatorname{Spec}A\), the
  affine categorical quotient is
  \(\operatorname{Spec}(A^G)\).
- If a finite locally free group scheme \(G\to S\) acts freely on an affine,
  quasi-affine, projective, or quasi-projective \(S\)-scheme \(X\), then the
  fppf quotient sheaf \(X/G\) is represented by a scheme and
  \(X\to X/G\) is an fppf \(G\)-torsor.  If \(G\to S\) is also etale, this
  torsor is finite etale.
- A general action may require an algebraic-space or stack quotient and is not
  forced into the category of schemes.

Accordingly, the general quotient functor lands in fppf sheaves, algebraic
spaces, or quotient stacks; the replete full subcategory on actions whose
fppf quotient is represented by a scheme supplies a scheme-valued quotient.
There is no single finite-group quotient construction promising every one of
these outputs.  The representability statement above is
[Stacks, Lemma 66.14.2](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/07S7).

## 10. Families and parameter loci

### 10.1 Families are objects of a slice

A family over \(B\) is an object \(X\to B\) of `Schemes(B)`.  The useful
replete full subcategories are defined by properties of this structure
morphism:

- flat and finitely presented;
- proper or projective;
- relative pure dimension \(d\);
- geometrically reduced, geometrically integral, or nodal fibers;
- smooth;
- local complete intersection;
- Cohen-Macaulay or Gorenstein.

A curve family is the join
\[
\mathsf{CurveFamilies}(B)
=
\mathsf{Schemes}(B).\mathsf{Flat}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(B).\mathsf{FinitePresentation}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(B).\mathsf{Separated}
\cap
\mathsf{Schemes}(B).\mathsf{RelativePureDimension}(1).
\]

Proper, projective, nodal, or stable curve families form further replete full
subcategories.
Fiberwise adjectives are not substituted for a family-level definition.

### 10.2 Linear systems and universal divisors

Let \(X\) be a \(k\)-scheme, \(L\) an invertible module, and
\(V\subseteq H^0(X,L)\) a finite-dimensional \(k\)-subspace.

The affine space \(\mathbf A(V)\) parameterizes actual sections, including the
zero section.  On \(X\times\mathbf A(V)\) there is a universal section of the
pullback of \(L\).

Use the line convention: \(\mathbf P(V)\) parameterizes one-dimensional
subspaces of \(V\), with tautological inclusion
\[
\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(V)}(-1)
\hookrightarrow
V\otimes\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(V)}.
\]
On \(X\times\mathbf P(V)\), evaluation gives a universal equation,
equivalently a section of
\[
p_X^*L\otimes p_{\mathbf P}^*\mathcal O_{\mathbf P(V)}(1)
\]
whose zero scheme, when regular, is the universal effective Cartier divisor.

The affine and projective parameter spaces are not interchangeable.  A
construction over \(\mathbf A(V)\) descends to \(\mathbf P(V)\) only after its
scaling behavior has been specified.

### 10.3 Singular subschemes of morphisms

Let \(D\to B\) be a flat, locally finitely presented family whose nonempty
fibers are equidimensional curves.  Its relative singular subscheme is
\[
\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)
=
V\!\left(\operatorname{Fitt}_1(\Omega_{D/B})\right)
\subseteq D.
\]

More generally, relative dimension \(d\) uses
\(\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{D/B})\).

For a point \(b\in B\), the underlying set of
\(\operatorname{Sing}(D_b/\kappa(b))\) is the locus where the fiber is not
smooth over \(\kappa(b)\).  It is the nonregular locus of \(D_b\) when
\(\kappa(b)\) is perfect.  Without that hypothesis, nonsmoothness over the
residue field and intrinsic nonregularity are not identified.

For a family of effective Cartier divisors in a smooth relative surface, this
agrees chartwise with the zero locus of the equation and its two relative
partial derivatives.  Base change of Fitting ideals gives:
\[
\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\times_BB'
\cong
\operatorname{Sing}(D\times_BB'/B').
\]

Thus the singular scheme of a fiber is obtained by fiber product, not by
rerunning an unrelated coordinate convention.

### 10.4 Standard parents for loci

For a parameter scheme \(P\), the architecture uses:

- `ClosedSubschemes(P)`;
- `OpenSubschemes(P)`;
- `LocallyClosedSubschemes(P)`;
- `ConstructibleSubsets(P)`.

`ConstructibleSubsets(P)` is a Boolean algebra under finite unions,
intersections, and complements.  A representation as a Boolean expression is
retained because equality of two presentations need not be easy to decide.

For \(f:X\to P\), distinguish:

1. the direct image subset \(f(|X|)\subseteq|P|\);
2. when \(f\) is of finite presentation, that same subset regarded by
   Chevalley's theorem as an element of `ConstructibleSubsets(P)`, together
   with a constructible presentation;
3. the scheme-theoretic image, the smallest closed subscheme through which
   \(f\) factors;
4. for a proper morphism, the fact that the direct image subset is closed.

The third construction exists for every scheme morphism.  Quasi-compactness
is the additional hypothesis under which its defining ideal is
\(\ker(\mathcal O_P\to f_*\mathcal O_X)\), is quasi-coherent, and restricts
to open subschemes of \(P\).

See:

- [Stacks, Scheme theoretic image](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/01R5);
- [Stacks, Chevalley's theorem](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/tag/054H).

Chevalley's theorem does not define a second image.  Under its hypotheses,
the direct image subset belongs to `ConstructibleSubsets(P)`.
Scheme-theoretic image remains a different construction because it retains
nilpotent structure.

### 10.5 Conditions with existential and uniqueness quantifiers

A condition such as "the fiber has a singular point away from the section
\(\sigma:B\to D\)" is represented by:

1. the relative singular subscheme \(\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\), whose
   underlying set is the nonsmooth locus;
2. the open complement
   \(\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\setminus\sigma(B)\);
3. its direct image in \(|B|\), represented in
   `ConstructibleSubsets(B)` by Chevalley's theorem.

The complement of that image is the set of \(b\in|B|\) for which the
geometric fiber over an algebraic closure of \(\kappa(b)\) has no singular
point outside the base-changed section.  Properness cannot be invoked after
removing the section, so the image is constructible in general, not
automatically closed.

This is the standard language for the global uniqueness condition in the
exactly-one-\(A_1\) problem.  It does not decide which Boolean expression,
equations, or decomposition will ultimately be most useful.

### 10.6 Finiteness of \(\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\to B\) and fiber length

Suppose

\[
b:\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\longrightarrow B
\]

is finite and finitely presented, and put

\[
\mathcal M=b_*\mathcal O_{\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)}.
\]

Then \(\mathcal M\) is a finitely presented
\(\mathcal O_B\)-module.  The locus on which \(\mathcal M\) is finite locally
free of rank \(r\) is determined locally by

\[
\operatorname{Fitt}_{r-1}(\mathcal M)=0,
\qquad
\operatorname{Fitt}_{r}(\mathcal M)=\mathcal O_B.
\]

On that open subscheme,
\(\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\to B\) is finite locally free of degree \(r\), so
every geometric fiber of the relative singular subscheme has scheme length
\(r\).

This statement concerns scheme length, including nilpotent structure.  It
does not by itself count the points in the support.  Equality between length
and the number of geometric support points requires the fiber to be reduced;
the case in which
\(\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\to B\) is finite etale is particularly clean.
Thus
Fitting ideals can describe loci on which the geometric fibers of
\(\operatorname{Sing}(D/B)\to B\) have constant scheme-theoretic length,
while a claim about exactly one geometric singular point still uses the
incidence morphism and its direct image.

## 11. Cyclic covers

### 11.1 Building data

Fix \(n\geq2\).  An object of
\(\mathsf{CyclicCoverData}_n\) is
\[
(Y,L,s),\qquad
L\in\mathsf{InvertibleModules}(Y),\quad
s\in H^0(Y,L^{\otimes n}).
\]

A morphism from \((Y',L',s')\) to \((Y,L,s)\) consists of a scheme morphism
\(f:Y'\to Y\) and an isomorphism
\[
\phi:L'\xrightarrow{\sim}f^*L
\]
carrying \(s'\) to \(f^*s\) under
\(\phi^{\otimes n}\).  These objects and morphisms form the category of the
stated cyclic-cover data.

### 11.2 Cover algebra and cover

The cover algebra is
\[
\mathcal A(L,s)
=
\mathcal O_Y\oplus L^{-1}\oplus\cdots\oplus L^{-(n-1)},
\]
with multiplication in degrees summing to at least \(n\) using the map
\[
L^{-n}\xrightarrow{s^\vee}\mathcal O_Y.
\]

The cover is
\[
X=\operatorname{Spec}_Y\mathcal A(L,s),
\qquad
\pi:X\to Y.
\]
Because \(\mathcal A(L,s)\) is finite locally free of rank \(n\),
\(\pi\) is finite locally free of degree \(n\).

There is a tautological section
\[
t\in H^0(X,\pi^*L),\qquad t^{\otimes n}=\pi^*s.
\]
The zero scheme \(B=Z(s)\) of the defining section exists for every \(s\).
If \(s\) is
regular, then \(B\) is an effective Cartier divisor, \(t\) is regular,
\(R=Z(t)\) is an effective Cartier divisor on \(X\), and
\[
\pi^*B=nR.
\]
Locally the cover is \(z^n=f\).  Hence
\[
\Omega_{X/Y}
\cong
\mathcal O_X\,dz/(n z^{n-1}dz).
\]
Under this regularity hypothesis, if \(n\) is invertible on \(Y\), the
ramification closed subscheme cut out by
\(\operatorname{Fitt}_0(\Omega_{X/Y})\) is the effective Cartier divisor
\((n-1)R\).

`CyclicCoverData.ParentMethods`:

- base scheme, degree, invertible module \(L\), and defining section;
- cover algebra;
- cover and covering morphism;
- deck \(\mu_n\)-action from the grading;
- tautological root section and the zero scheme of the defining section;
- the effective Cartier zero divisors and ramification divisor on the
  replete full subcategories specified above.

`MorphismMethods`:

- underlying base morphism;
- compatible pullback isomorphism;
- induced algebra homomorphism;
- induced morphism of covers;
- compatibility with deck actions.

### 11.3 Full subcategories defined by properties and categories of additional data

The following predicates define replete full subcategories of
`CyclicCoverData(n)`:

- data for which the defining section \(s\) is regular;
- data for which \(s\) is regular and \(Z(s)\) is smooth over the base;
- data for which \(s\) is regular and \(Z(s)\) is reduced;
- normal cover;
- smooth cover.

Equivariant building data do not define a full subcategory: they form a
category of additional data consisting of an action on the base, a
linearization of \(L\), and the required equivariance of
the defining section.  A named fixed subscheme is likewise additional data.
Its scheme-theoretic intersection with \(Z(s)\), the condition that this
intersection be empty, and local conditions on \(Z(s)\) at its points are
retained as distinct constructions and predicates.  No generic notion of
"transversality to the fixed subscheme" is introduced.

If \(n\) is invertible on \(Y\), the cover is etale over
\(Y\setminus Z(s)\).  More precisely, if \(Y\to S\) is smooth, \(n\) is
invertible on \(S\), and \(Z(s)\subset Y\) is a smooth relative effective
Cartier divisor, then the local equation \(z^n=f\) and the relative Jacobian
criterion show that \(X\to S\) is smooth.

The deck group is the group scheme \(\mu_n\).  It is not silently replaced by
the constant group scheme associated to \(\mathbf Z/n\mathbf Z\) when the two
are not canonically isomorphic over the base.

The building-data construction and its relation to branch data are the cyclic
case of [Pardini, *Abelian covers of algebraic varieties*](https://doi.org/10.1515/crll.1991.417.191).

### 11.4 Double covers and canonical modules

For \(n=2\),
\[
\mathcal A=\mathcal O_Y\oplus L^{-1}.
\]
Over a field of characteristic different from \(2\), if \(Y\) and the branch
divisor are smooth, then \(X\) is smooth and
\[
\omega_X\cong\pi^*(\omega_Y\otimes L).
\]

For
\[
Y=\mathbf P^1\times\mathbf P^1,\qquad
L=\mathcal O_Y(2,2),
\]
the right side is trivial.  If the branch divisor is smooth of bidegree
\((4,4)\), the resulting double cover is a smooth projective surface with
trivial canonical bundle.  Moreover,
\[
\pi_*\mathcal O_X
\cong
\mathcal O_Y\oplus\mathcal O_Y(-2,-2),
\]
so Kunneth's formula gives \(H^1(X,\mathcal O_X)=0\).  Thus this later
construction is a K3 surface.

An action of a group on \(Y\) lifts only after the building data are made
equivariant.  A linearization of \(L\) must carry \(s\) with the required
character.  The possible lifts and their fixed subschemes belong to the
equivariant cyclic-cover category.

### 12.1 Functors indexed by external morphisms

The following operations cross from a parent over one base object to a parent
over another.  Their mathematical owner is the morphism in the first column.

| Indexing morphism | Induced functor or operation | Hypotheses and codomain |
| --- | --- | --- |
| \(\alpha:R\to S\) | extension \(I\mapsto IS\) and contraction \(J\mapsto\alpha^{-1}(J)\) of ideals | `Ideals(R)` to `Ideals(S)` for extension and the reverse direction for contraction |
| \(\alpha:R\to S\) | scalar extension \(M\mapsto M\otimes_RS\) | `Modules(R)` to `Modules(S)` |
| \(\alpha:R\to S\) | algebra base change \(A\mapsto A\otimes_RS\) | `CommutativeAlgebras(R)` to `CommutativeAlgebras(S)` |
| \(\alpha:R\to S\) | opposite affine-scheme morphism \(\operatorname{Spec}S\to\operatorname{Spec}R\) | no additional hypothesis |
| \(f:X\to Y\) | \(f^*:\mathsf{QCoh}(Y)\to\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\), including pullback of invertible modules, their Picard classes, and sections | every scheme morphism |
| \(f:X\to Y\) | \(f_*:\mathsf{QCoh}(X)\to\mathsf{QCoh}(Y)\) | \(f\) quasi-compact and quasi-separated |
| \(f:X\to Y\) | pullback of effective Cartier divisors | the subcategories of scheme morphisms in Section 6.4 on which pullback preserves regular local equations, including flat morphisms |
| \(f:X\to Y\) | inverse image on constructible subsets; direct image with a constructible presentation | inverse image is general; the direct-image statement uses Chevalley's hypotheses, here finite presentation |
| \(u:S'\to S\) | base change \(\mathsf{Schemes}(S)\to\mathsf{Schemes}(S')\) and its restrictions to affine, projective, smooth, proper, flat, and finite morphism categories | the stated morphism properties are stable under arbitrary base change |
| \(u:B'\to B\) | base change of gluing data, actions, families, embedded schemes, blowup data, and cyclic-cover data | each construction lands in the strongest subcategory preserved by the relevant base-change theorem; blowups commute with flat base change in the form used here |

This table does not create new morphisms in `QCoh(X)`, `Ideals(R)`,
`PicardGroup(X)`, or any other fixed-base parent.  It records functors between
such parents indexed by an already named ring or scheme morphism.

### 12.2 Where abstract methods are appropriate

An abstract category method is justified when membership promises an actual
mathematical operation needed by every object of the category.

Examples:

- every local ring supplies its unique maximal ideal and residue map;
- every \(S\)-scheme supplies its structure morphism;
- every action supplies its action morphism;
- every choice of gluing data supplies its affine charts, overlap maps, and
  cocycle;
- every cyclic-cover datum supplies its invertible module \(L\) and defining
  section.

A property alone need not force a new data-returning method.  A reduced scheme
does not need to supply a history of how reducedness was established.  Its
category with the `Reduced` axiom supplies consequences of reducedness and
the predicate inherited from category membership.

### 12.3 Functorial rollup

Operations depending only on data preserved by a projection or forgetful
functor belong to the first general category that defines them:

- local rings use the forgetful functor to commutative rings for ring
  arithmetic;
- \(R\)-algebras use the forgetful functor to commutative rings for ideals and
  quotients;
- pointed schemes use the forgetful functor to schemes for open and closed
  subschemes;
- embedded schemes use their underlying scheme for intrinsic geometry and
  their embedding arrow for coordinate geometry;
- equivariant objects use the underlying object for nonequivariant
  operations;
- cyclic-cover data use invertible-module and relative-spectrum functors.

This keeps one mathematical definition for each operation while preserving
the structure needed by the specialized category.

## 13. The mathematician-facing surface

The intended surface should read like ordinary algebraic geometry.

### 13.1 Quotients and local rings

```sage
R.<x, y> = PolynomialRing(QQ)
I = R.ideal(x*y)
A, q = R.quotient(I, map=True)

p = A.ideal(x, y).as_prime()
O, loc = A.localization_at_prime(p, map=True)

```

The mathematical types are visible:

\operatorname{loc}:A\to\mathcal O_{C,p},\qquad
\mathcal O_{C,p}\to\kappa(p).
\]

### 13.2 Schemes and local maps

```sage
X = Spec(A)
xp = X.point(p)
Xgerm = X.local_scheme(xp)

Y = Spec(B)
f = Hom(X, Y)(...)
ygerm_map = f.induced_local_map(at=xp)
```

`xp` is a topological point with its canonical point morphism.  A
\(T\)-valued point is instead an element of `Hom(T, X)`.

### 13.3 Invertible modules and divisors

```sage
P1 = ProjectiveSpace(QQ, 1)
Y = P1.product(P1)
pr1, pr2 = Y.projections()

L = pr1.pullback(P1.O(4)).tensor(pr2.pullback(P1.O(4)))
V = L.global_sections()
s = V(...)

Z = s.zero_scheme()
D = s.effective_cartier_divisor()   # available when s is regular
```

The pullback of \(\mathcal O(4)\) along either projection lands in
`InvertibleModules(Y)`.  The bidegree notation is a standard abbreviation for
this construction.

### 13.4 Actions and invariant sections

```sage
tau = Hom(Y, Y)(...)
rho = C2.action_on(Y, generator=tau)
F = rho.fixed_subscheme()

Ltau = rho.linearize(L, generator_isomorphism=...)
representation = Ltau.representation_on_global_sections()
Vplus = representation.invariants()
Vminus = representation.eigenspace(-1)
```

The representation is obtained from the linearized invertible module, not
from the automorphism of \(Y\) alone.  Here the ground field is
\(\mathbf Q\), so the involution acts semisimply and the \(+1\) and \(-1\)
eigenspaces give the full decomposition.

### 13.5 Families and singular incidence

```sage
P = Vplus.projective_space()
C = universal_effective_cartier_divisor(Y, Ltau, Vplus, over=P)
Sing = C.relative_singular_subscheme()

p = p00
evp = Vplus.evaluation_map(at=p)
Hp = P.closed_subscheme(evp)

s_univ = C.restrict_over(Hp).defining_section()
sigma_p = p.constant_section(over=Hp)
j2_p = sigma_p.pullback(s_univ.jet(order=2))
q_p = j2_p.degree_two_term()
disc_q = q_p.discriminant()
local_node = disc_q.nonvanishing_locus()

sigma_p = sigma_p.restrict_over(local_node)
additional_singularities = (
    Sing.restrict_over(local_node).open_complement_of(sigma_p.image())
)
bad_parameters = (
    additional_singularities.structure_morphism().constructible_image()
)
exactly_one_node_at_p = local_node.constructible_difference(bad_parameters)
```

The displayed operations have the following mathematical types:

- `Hp` is a closed parameter subscheme;
- `q_p` is the degree-two term of the restricted second jet in
  \(\operatorname{Sym}^2(\sigma_p^*\Omega_{Y\times H_p/H_p})
  \otimes\sigma_p^*L_{\mathrm{univ}}\); its availability uses the vanishing
  of the pulled-back first jet forced by invariance at the fixed point;
- `disc_q` is a section of
  \((\det\sigma_p^*\Omega_{Y\times H_p/H_p})^{\otimes2}
  \otimes(\sigma_p^*L_{\mathrm{univ}})^{\otimes2}\);
- `local_node` is the open nondegenerate-quadratic locus in `Hp`;
- `additional_singularities` is the open complement of the distinguished
  section in the relative singular subscheme;
- `bad_parameters` is its direct image, returned with a constructible
  presentation by Chevalley's theorem;
- the final result is a constructible subset unless the investigation proves
  that it is open, locally closed, or closed and supplies the corresponding
  presentation.

Principal parts and jets belong to the defining invertible module on the
smooth relative surface.  The degree-two term belongs to the category in which
the first jet along the section vanishes.  Its discriminant and nonvanishing
locus require relative cotangent rank two and \(2\) invertible on the base.
They are not methods on every family, and they do not require a chosen chart
or local Hessian.

### 13.6 Cyclic covers

## 14. Specialization to invariant \((4,4)\)-curves

Let
\[
Y=\mathbf P^1\times\mathbf P^1,\qquad
L=\mathcal O_Y(4,4),
\]
and
\[
\tau([x_0:x_1],[y_0:y_1])
=
([x_0:-x_1],[y_0:-y_1]).
\]

Choose the standard linearization of \(L\).  A monomial with exponents
\((i,j)\) in \(x_1,y_1\) has eigenvalue \((-1)^{i+j}\), so
\[
H^0(Y,L)=V_+\oplus V_-,
\qquad
\dim V_+=3\cdot3+2\cdot2=13,
\qquad
\dim V_-=12.
\]

The fixed subscheme is the four coordinate corners.  For its standard
linearization, the fibers of \(L\) at these corners have trivial character,
so evaluation of anti-invariant sections vanishes there.

Fix a corner \(p\) and regular parameters \(u,v\) in which
\[
\tau(u,v)=(-u,-v).
\]
An invariant local equation has only terms of even total degree.  Hence an
invariant curve through \(p\) has no linear term and is singular at \(p\).
The equation \(s(p)=0\) therefore defines a hyperplane
\[
H_p\subset\mathbf P(V_+).
\]
Inside \(H_p\), nondegeneracy of the quadratic term is an open condition and
gives a geometric node in characteristic different from \(2\).

This local calculation does not settle the global problem.  A curve may:

- be singular at another fixed corner;
- have a pair \(q,\tau(q)\) of singular points away from the fixed scheme;
- be nonreduced or reducible;
- have a worse singularity at \(p\).

The global condition is therefore expressed through the universal relative
singular scheme, the open complement of the distinguished section, and the
direct image of that open complement, known by Chevalley's theorem to be
constructible.  The exact useful description of the resulting locus remains
a question for the investigation, as specified in
[H0_INVARIANT_CURVE_RESEARCH_SPECIFICATION.md](H0_INVARIANT_CURVE_RESEARCH_SPECIFICATION.md).

## 16. Mathematical dependency boundaries

The architecture is governed by the following statements.

1. A Sage category with an axiom is used only for a replete full subcategory
   with unchanged homsets.
2. Additional data or stricter morphisms produce a category of the stated
   objects and arrows, with a projection or forgetful functor which need not
   be faithful.
3. Parameterized properties retain their parameter in the category.
4. A constructor returns its result in every full subcategory implied by its
   mathematical input.
5. Recognition from arbitrary data is offered only where a decision procedure
   is known on a stated mathematical category.  A finite presentation may be
   computation input, but it does not become the home of the predicate being
   decided.
6. Scheme points, \(T\)-valued points, and geometric points remain distinct.
7. Relative properties live first on scheme morphisms; the corresponding
   replete full subcategories of \(S\)-schemes are obtained from the
   structure morphism.
8. Reduced and geometrically reduced are distinct because base change can
   create nilpotents.
9. The smooth locus is an open subscheme.  Under the flat,
    locally-finitely-presented, equidimensional-fiber hypotheses, the
    relative singular subscheme is the closed subscheme cut out by
    \(\operatorname{Fitt}_d(\Omega_{X/S})\), and its underlying set is the
    nonsmooth locus.
10. Pullback of invertible modules is general.  Pullback of effective Cartier
    divisors requires preservation of regularity.
11. A section, its zero scheme, and an effective Cartier divisor are distinct
    until regularity relates them.
12. The Picard group, Cartier divisor class group, and Weil divisor class
    group are not identified without hypotheses.
13. Coordinate ideals belong to schemes together with a chosen embedding and
    coordinate presentation, not to a bare abstract scheme.
14. Curves and surfaces are joins of scheme categories, not unrelated class
    hierarchies.
15. A singularity type belongs to a category of local schemes or affine
    formal schemes with its field and equivalence relation fixed.
16. The relative singular subscheme and its fibers are connected by base
    change.  On fibers over perfect fields its underlying set is the
    nonregular locus; no such identification is made over an arbitrary
    imperfect field.
17. Closed, open, locally closed, and constructible subsets remain distinct;
    under Chevalley's hypotheses the direct image subset belongs to
    `ConstructibleSubsets(P)`, whereas scheme-theoretic image is a separate
    construction.
18. The fixed subscheme of one automorphism is its equalizer with the
    identity; finite abstract groups use the scheme-theoretic intersection of
    these equalizers, while a group-scheme action first supplies a fixed-point
    functor and requires representability before it supplies a scheme.
19. A representation on sections requires a linearization.
20. A cyclic cover is built from \((L,s)\); geometric conclusions use the
    replete full subcategories defined by conditions on the branch divisor
    and group action.
21. The architecture fixes the parameter scheme, incidence morphisms, local
    conditions, and available subset categories for the exactly-one-\(A_1\)
    locus; it does not prescribe that locus's eventual best presentation.
